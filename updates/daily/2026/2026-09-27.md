# Daily PM Research Update: 2026-09-27

## The Short Version

Two threads worth connecting today: Karri Saarinen asks whether AI-accelerated output is quietly eroding the organizational learning that made teams great, and Cursor ships Projects, a feature that lets agents take on months-long bodies of work with persistent context. One is a philosophical challenge, the other is the product answer to it. Both are worth sitting with.

---

### Karri Saarinen - Tools Are Getting Better. Are We?

**Source:** https://x.com/karrisaarinen/status/2103512094981419349
**Credibility:** High (Karri Saarinen is CEO of Linear, deeply embedded in how product teams use AI tooling, first-person reflection)

**What happened:** Karri posted a sharp observation on September 25: product work has always produced two things simultaneously, the product itself and the team's accumulated understanding of how to build better products. His concern is that when AI agents absorb more of the doing, the team's learning loop may break. Great companies, he argues, compound their understanding over time. If you delegate the work that generates the understanding, you might be shipping faster toward a knowledge ceiling you didn't know you were building.

**Key patterns:**
- The "two outputs" framing is precise and useful. Every sprint produces (1) a shipped thing and (2) the team's improved judgment about what to ship next. These are not separable in traditional development. AI agents may decouple them.
- "Great companies compound their understanding" is the core claim. It's not about individual productivity but organizational learning rate.
- The concern is not about AI tools specifically but about the specific way teams are choosing to use them: maximizing throughput rather than preserving the conditions that generate learning.

**Why it matters for PMs:** This is a real product leadership question disguised as a philosophical one. If your engineers are reviewing AI-generated PRs rather than writing code, what are they learning? If your designers are directing AI to generate variants rather than going through the craft of layout decisions, what intuitions are they building? The throughput metrics will look great. The judgment atrophy won't show up in a dashboard. For PMs, the risk is compounded: PM work is already abstracted from implementation, and AI tools make it easier to generate specs, strategies, and frameworks without doing the underlying thinking that makes those artifacts useful. The output looks the same. The understanding underneath it doesn't build the same way.

**Critical questions:**
- Is organizational learning actually captured in the doing, or is it captured in reviewing, deciding, and iterating? If agents do the doing but humans still review and decide, does the learning transfer?
- Is this concern most acute for junior team members who haven't yet built deep intuitions, versus senior people who can meaningfully direct agents from existing mental models?
- What does a team that deliberately preserves learning loops alongside AI acceleration actually look like structurally? Is anyone doing this intentionally?
- Could observability tooling (LangSmith, etc.) provide a new form of learning by making agent decisions inspectable in ways code reviews never were?

**Action you could take today:** In your next sprint retro, add one question: "What did we learn about our users or our system that we didn't know before?" If nobody has a good answer, that's a signal worth acting on.

### Cursor - Projects: Persistent Context for Long-Horizon Work

**Source:** https://cursor.com/changelog/projects
**Credibility:** High (first-party changelog, shipped product)

**What happened:** Cursor launched Projects on September 10, and it's a meaningful shift in what "AI coding assistant" means as a category. Projects lets teams take on large bodies of work — a feature, a migration, a full app — with context that persists over months. The agent delegates tasks to subagents, performs recurring work autonomously, and doesn't require intervention at each loop. This is the difference between an AI tool that helps you write code and an AI system that owns a unit of work.

**Key technical details:**
- Persistent context maintained across the full lifecycle of a project (weeks to months), not just a session
- Task delegation to thousands of subagents — designed to parallelize work at a scale no human team can match
- Recurring work execution without per-loop human prompting
- Works with or without a connected GitHub repo (the earlier "Start from scratch" update enabled repo-free cloud agents)
- Self-hosted machines option (launched Sep 2) means enterprise teams can keep codebase, build outputs, and secrets entirely inside their own infrastructure

**Why it matters for PMs:** The build-vs-buy calculus for software features is changing faster than most roadmap processes can track. If a Cursor Project can handle a migration or a greenfield feature with persistent context and parallel subagents, the cost and time assumptions behind "we need a sprint to build this" are no longer reliable. More immediately: Cursor is now competing in a different category than it was six months ago. It's not a coding assistant. It's a software production system. For PMs at companies evaluating or already using Cursor, the question shifts from "does this make engineers faster?" to "what kind of work can we fully delegate to this, and what governance do we need around that?"

**Critical questions:**
- Persistent context over months sounds powerful but also fragile. What happens when the project context drifts from the actual codebase state? How does Cursor handle context invalidation?
- "Delegates tasks to thousands of subagents" — what does error propagation look like at that scale? If a subagent makes a wrong architectural decision in week 2, how visible is that to the team reviewing in week 6?
- The self-hosted machines feature is clearly targeted at enterprise security requirements. What compliance certifications or audit trails come with it?
- Does Projects change the role of the senior engineer on a team, or mainly accelerate what junior engineers can take on?

**Action you could take today:** Pull up your current roadmap and flag two to three items that are defined as "engineering-heavy" or "too complex for this quarter." Ask whether the constraint is still the engineering time, or whether that assumption was made before tools like Cursor Projects existed.

### Microsoft - AI Agent Risk Discovery at Runtime

**Source:** https://commandline.microsoft.com/run-assert-eval-responsible-ai-agent-risk-discovery-at-runtime/
**Credibility:** Medium (first-party Microsoft blog, but title-only synthesis — full article at URL)

**What happened:** Microsoft published a post on September 25 describing a new skill that finds AI agent risks at runtime, applies fixes, and then verifies the fix worked. The framing is notable: this is not a pre-deployment eval, it's a runtime intervention. The three-step loop (find, fix, prove) suggests a pattern for responsible AI that's closer to how observability works in production systems than how most teams currently think about AI safety.

**Key patterns:**
- Runtime risk discovery rather than pre-deployment testing shifts AI safety from a gate to a continuous process
- The "assert" step (verifying the fix worked) is the piece most AI safety tooling skips. Fixing something without verifying the fix is just hope.
- Positions this as a "skill" — suggesting it's composable and can be attached to other agent workflows, not a standalone product

**Why it matters for PMs:** If you're building or buying agentic products, this is the architectural question you haven't been asked yet: what's your runtime safety story? Pre-launch evals catch a lot, but agents in production encounter situations that evals didn't cover. The find-fix-assert loop is a pattern worth understanding and potentially requiring from your vendors.

**Critical questions:**
- What kinds of risks does this actually catch? Agent hallucinations? Unsafe tool use? Prompt injection? The title doesn't specify.
- How does runtime intervention affect latency and user experience? If the agent pauses to assess its own risk mid-task, what does that look like to the user?
- Is this available as an API or SDK pattern, or only embedded in Microsoft's own agent infrastructure?

**Action you could take today:** Look at the agentic features in your product (or ones you're planning). Write down what your current runtime safety mechanism is. If the answer is "evals before launch," decide whether that's sufficient for what the agent actually does in production.

## The Thread

**AI is accelerating output and quietly raising questions about what gets preserved.** Karri's post about organizational learning, Cursor Projects taking on months of autonomous work, and Microsoft's runtime risk finder all point at the same tension: AI makes it easy to move faster, but the things that used to slow us down (writing the code, making the judgment call, catching the bug before it ships) were also the things that built team capability and product intuition. The tooling is maturing. The question of what to do with the reclaimed time and cognitive space is not.

## Sit With This
