### Cursor — Self-Hosted Machines for Cloud Agents

**Source:** https://cursor.com/changelog/self-hosted-machines
**Credibility:** High (first-party changelog)

**What happened:** Cursor shipped self-hosted machines support for Cloud Agents. Tool execution now stays entirely inside your own network. The codebase, build outputs, and secrets never leave your infrastructure — the agent handles tool calls locally while Cursor manages orchestration. This is a direct response to the enterprise security objection that's been blocking adoption of agentic coding tools in larger organizations.

**Key technical details:**
- Tool execution runs on internal machines in your own infrastructure
- Codebase, build outputs, and secrets stay on your network
- Agent handles tool calls locally rather than routing through Cursor's cloud
- Designed for orgs with strict data residency or compliance requirements
- Works alongside Cursor Origin (their code hosting) for teams that want the full stack

**Why it matters for PMs:** This is the unlock that enterprise engineering orgs have been waiting for. The standard objection to agentic coding tools at large companies isn't price or capability — it's "we can't send our IP to a third-party cloud." Cursor just answered that objection directly. If you're a PM evaluating developer tooling for a regulated industry (fintech, healthcare, defense), this changes the conversation. The self-hosted option is also a classic enterprise land-and-expand signal: get the security team to sign off, then expand usage to more teams.

**Critical questions:**
- What's the operational burden of maintaining self-hosted machines? Teams that want agent benefits but not infrastructure headaches may find this trades one problem for another.
- Does self-hosted execution limit which models or capabilities are available, or does Cursor's full agent capability set work the same way?
- How does this compare to GitHub Copilot's enterprise offering on data isolation? If the security story is equivalent, differentiation shifts back to product experience.
- Is this priced differently from standard Cloud Agents? Enterprise pricing usually comes with margin that funds the sales cycle, which is worth watching.

**Action you could take today:** If you're in a sales or evaluation conversation with an enterprise engineering team that cited security as a blocker, send them this changelog entry. If you're on the product side building tools for enterprise teams, audit your data handling documentation — Cursor just raised the bar on transparency about where data goes.

### Teresa Torres — AI Evals: A Hands-On Guide for Product Teams

**Source:** https://www.producttalk.org/ai-evals/ and https://www.producttalk.org/ai-evals-test-harness/
**Credibility:** High (Teresa Torres is a recognized PM craft expert; this is a two-part guide with accompanying tooling)

**What happened:** Teresa Torres published a comprehensive AI evals guide for product teams alongside a practical test harness for running experiments. The guide covers error analysis, the four types of evals, and how to run your first experiment. The companion post gives you the actual tool she uses so you can skip the setup overhead and start running evals immediately. This is exactly the kind of PM-specific, hands-on resource that usually doesn't exist — most evals content is written for ML engineers, not product teams.

**Key capabilities / patterns:**
- Four types of evals framework (presumably covering correctness, safety, quality, and user experience dimensions — typical in this space)
- Error analysis approach: start with understanding failure modes before building evals
- Test harness designed for baseline runs: you have to run every item to establish a baseline before you can measure improvement
- Built for product teams, not ML teams — the framing is around product decisions, not model training
- Companion tooling included, not just theory

**Why it matters for PMs:** Most product teams shipping AI features are flying blind on quality. They ship, they watch support tickets, they hope. Evals give you a feedback loop that's faster and more systematic than support tickets or NPS. Torres building this for product teams specifically means the entry point is much lower than the ML-native evals tooling (Braintrust, Arize, etc.). If you're shipping an AI feature right now and you don't have a structured way to measure whether it's getting better or worse, this is where to start. The open question from `context/open-questions.md` about how user expectations for AI quality are evolving makes this especially relevant — evals are how you know if you're keeping up.

**Critical questions:**
- How do you choose what to evaluate when you don't yet know what failure modes matter most? The guide likely covers this, but it's the hardest part to do well.
- Evals require ground truth or human judgment. Where does that come from for product teams without ML infrastructure? What's the minimum viable eval pipeline before it's worth the investment?
- How do Torres's four eval types map to the specific AI feature types most product teams are building (summarization, Q&A, classification, generation)? The framework may need translation to apply cleanly.
- At what product maturity stage do evals become worth the overhead? Early prototype vs. scaling to 100k users probably need different approaches.

**Action you could take today:** Read the evals guide (https://www.producttalk.org/ai-evals/), then download the test harness. Pick one AI feature you're currently running and define what "wrong" looks like — just that one step will tell you a lot about whether you actually know what good output is.

### Julie Zhuo — You Cannot Mandate an AI Transformation

**Source:** https://joulee.medium.com/you-cannot-mandate-an-ai-transformation-e2513cc3bbcc
**Credibility:** High (Julie Zhuo is a former VP Design at Facebook, author of The Making of a Manager, and a well-regarded product and leadership voice)

**What happened:** Julie Zhuo published a piece arguing that top-down mandates don't drive AI transformation — behavior change does. The title is the argument: you can tell teams to use AI, but you can't mandate the curiosity, experimentation, and workflow changes that make AI actually stick. (The full article text wasn't in the collected data, but the argument is clearly in the headline and Zhuo's consistent voice on organizational change.)

**Key patterns to note:**
- Transformation is behavioral, not directive — the same pattern that made digital transformation mandates fail in the 2010s applies here
- Adoption has to be intrinsically motivated (curiosity, seeing real value) not extrinsically mandated (policy compliance)
- Leaders who've succeeded with AI adoption at team level typically modeled the behavior themselves rather than requiring it
- The implication: your role as a PM is to create the conditions for people to want to adopt AI, not to build adoption into OKRs

**Why it matters for PMs:** If you're in an org where AI adoption is becoming a mandate (and most of you are), this reframes the problem. The question isn't "how do we get our team to use AI tools" — it's "what conditions would make them want to?" That's a product thinking question, not a policy question. For PMs specifically, this also applies to how you build AI features: you can't mandate users to trust AI, you have to earn it with good experience design and reliable output quality.

**Critical questions:**
- What does "creating conditions" actually look like in practice? Zhuo may have specifics, but the abstract version of this argument is easy to agree with and hard to act on.
- Is there a meaningful difference between mandates that fail and strong cultural expectations that succeed? The line between "we expect this" and "you must do this" isn't always clear in org culture.
- For PMs building AI products: does this mean you should optimize for voluntary adoption signals (engagement, sharing, return visits) over compliance metrics in your AI feature instrumentation?

**Action you could take today:** If your team or org is tracking AI tool usage as a metric, spend 10 minutes writing down what behavior you actually want to see — and whether measuring usage actually correlates to it. Usage compliance and genuine adoption are not the same thing, and conflating them is a classic measurement trap.
