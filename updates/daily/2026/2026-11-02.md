# Daily PM Research Update: 2026-11-02

## The Short Version

Notion rebuilt its AI pricing around a credit system right as LangChain shipped managed infrastructure for the exact kind of long-running agent that makes credit-based pricing necessary in the first place, and Figma's PM team showed what it actually looks like to run 60+ PMs on a shared AI skill repo instead of everyone freelancing their own prompts.

## Notion - Agents Rebuilt to "Do More for Less," New Credit System Replaces Flat AI Pricing

**Source:** https://www.notion.com/blog/notion-ai-more-for-less
**Credibility:** High (first-party product announcement from Notion co-founder)

**What happened:** Notion shipped a reworked AI system built around smarter model routing, long-running agents, bring-your-own-AI (BYO-AI) options, and a new usage-based credit system. Akshay Kothari, Notion's co-founder, announced the change directly, framing it as a shift from flat-rate AI access to a metered model that reflects actual compute cost per task.

**Key technical details:**
- Smarter model routing picks the right model for the task rather than always defaulting to the most expensive option (companion piece from Engineering Manager Ghita Houir Alami details the routing/judging system using pairwise comparisons and calibrated judging to measure model quality at scale)
- Long-running agents are now a first-class capability, not a one-off chat response
- Bring-your-own-AI lets customers plug in their own model/API keys instead of using Notion's bundled credits
- New credit system replaces however AI usage was previously bundled into subscription tiers

**Why it matters for PMs:** This is the tension every PM shipping AI features eventually hits: flat pricing is great for adoption and terrible for unit economics once usage includes long-running agentic tasks instead of quick completions. Notion's answer, credits plus BYO-AI plus model routing, is a three-part hedge: credits cap your downside, BYO-AI lets power users route around your margin problem entirely, and routing is the lever you pull to protect margin without the user noticing. The model router piece (published alongside this) is the part worth studying closely, pairwise comparison and calibrated judging is a legitimate way to validate "we switched your query to a cheaper model and you didn't notice," and that's the exact claim PMs get challenged on internally.

**Critical questions:**
- Does bring-your-own-AI cannibalize Notion's own AI revenue among its most technical, highest-usage customers, exactly the segment companies usually want to monetize hardest?
- What happens to casual AI users who now have to think about credits for tasks that used to feel unlimited? Does metering kill the habitual usage that made AI features sticky?
- How transparent is Notion being about which tasks get routed to cheaper models, and does that quality variance show up for users in ways that erode trust?

**Action you could take today:** If your product bundles AI usage into a flat subscription, pull your actual cost-per-user data for your top 10% of AI users versus your median user. If the gap is more than 5x, you have the same problem Notion just solved for and should be modeling a credit or BYO path now, not after a cost crisis forces it.

### LangChain - Managed Deep Agents Goes to Private Beta, Now Includes Browser Use

**Source:** https://www.langchain.com/blog/introducing-managed-deep-agents and https://www.langchain.com/blog/browser-use-in-managed-deep-agents
**Credibility:** High (first-party product launch from LangChain, corroborated by CEO Harrison Chase's own post on the same feature)

**What happened:** LangChain launched Managed Deep Agents in private beta, a hosted runtime for production deep agents that bundles durable execution, sandboxes, tool access, and LangSmith observability so teams don't have to build the agent runtime themselves. The same week, LangChain added browser use as a built-in capability, letting agents operate inside a real browser for tasks like travel booking or subscription cancellation, with a handoff mechanism so a human can step in to sign in when needed.

**Key technical details:**
- Durable execution means agent runs can survive failures, restarts, or long wait times without losing state, the thing that makes "long-running agent" actually mean something rather than a chat session that times out
- Sandboxes isolate agent execution for safety, paired with tool access and LangSmith observability baked in rather than bolted on
- Browser use is described by Harrison Chase as "one of the fastest growing agent primitives," with context-efficient tooling specifically tuned for browser tasks
- A human handoff flow exists for authentication steps, the agent doesn't try to guess your password, it hands control back to you

**Why it matters for PMs:** This is LangChain explicitly packaging the infrastructure tax that every team building a "deep agent" product currently pays themselves: durable execution, sandboxing, observability, and now browser control. If you're deciding whether to build agent infrastructure in-house or buy it, this launch changes the math, especially the browser use piece, which until now meant stitching together Playwright or similar tooling and hoping it held up in production. The human handoff pattern for credential steps is also worth noting as a default UX pattern: it's the same trust boundary Instinct and Meta Muse are each solving differently in the consumer agent space.

**Critical questions:**
- What's the latency and cost profile of a managed deep agent running a browser session compared to a direct API-based agent? Browser automation is historically slow and brittle.
- How does LangSmith observability actually surface browser-based agent failures, screenshots, DOM state, something else? Debugging a stuck browser agent is a different problem than debugging a bad tool call.
- Private beta means limited availability, what's the actual wait list and timeline to GA, and should teams build a stopgap in the meantime?

**Action you could take today:** If your team has an internal "should we build or buy agent infrastructure" debate going, add Managed Deep Agents to the bake-off list this week, specifically test the browser use primitive against whatever custom browser automation you're currently running or considering.

### Lenny Rachitsky / Ezra Mechaber (Figma) - How Figma's PMs Actually Use AI Every Day: PM OS and AutoXP

**Source:** https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-figmas-pms-actually-use-ai-every
**Credibility:** High (direct practitioner interview, named PM describing shipped internal tooling)

**What happened:** Figma growth PM Ezra Mechaber went on Lenny's podcast and demoed two internal systems: "PM OS," a 31-skill repo used by more than 60 PMs at Figma, and "AutoXP," an agent that runs growth experiments autonomously with a single human checkpoint before launch.

**Key technical details:**
- PM OS is a shared repository of 31 distinct "skills" (reusable AI workflows/prompts), standardized across the PM org rather than each PM building their own one-off prompts
- 60+ PMs actively use the shared repo, suggesting this moved past a pet project into actual organizational infrastructure
- AutoXP runs growth experiments with AI handling the setup/execution and a single human checkpoint, meaning a PM reviews and approves before the experiment goes live rather than babysitting every step

**Why it matters for PMs:** This is the team-level AI adoption pattern worth copying directly: instead of every PM individually prompting ChatGPT and reinventing the wheel, Figma standardized AI workflows into a shared, versioned skill library. That's the same "individual tool becomes team infrastructure" shift Wispr Flow made with Team Dictionary, except here it's happening inside a product org's own workflow, not inside a vendor's product. AutoXP's one-checkpoint model is also a concrete, specific answer to the "how much autonomy should an agent have" question: fully autonomous execution, but a mandatory human gate before anything external-facing happens.

**Critical questions:**
- What happens when a skill in PM OS is wrong or outdated, who owns maintaining a 31-skill shared repo, and how often does it get revised?
- Is the single checkpoint in AutoXP actually catching bad experiments, or has it become a rubber stamp because the agent is usually right and reviewers have stopped scrutinizing closely?
- How much of this is replicable without Figma's internal engineering support to build and maintain AutoXP versus being a one-off power move by a well-resourced growth team?

**Action you could take today:** Audit how many different ad hoc AI prompts exist across your PM team for the same five or six recurring tasks (competitive analysis, PRD drafts, experiment design). If it's more than one version per task, start a shared skill doc this week, you don't need an agent to get the first 80% of the benefit Figma is describing.

## The Thread

**Shared infrastructure is eating individual AI workflows, on both the vendor and the user-org side.** Notion turned individual AI usage into a metered, routed system; LangChain packaged the deep-agent runtime so teams stop building it themselves; and Figma's PM team built an internal shared skill repo instead of leaving 60 PMs to each freelance their own prompts. The pattern all week: ad hoc, individual AI usage is giving way to standardized, shared, often metered infrastructure, whether that infrastructure is built by a vendor or built in-house.
