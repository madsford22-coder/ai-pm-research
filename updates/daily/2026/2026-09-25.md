# Daily PM Research Update: 2026-09-25

## The Short Version

Microsoft just rebuilt Copilot from scratch around three modes — Home, Code, and Autopilot — and called it "a new OS for work." Julie Zhuo is asking a sharper question: why hasn't personal AI actually gone mass market yet? Both items are really about the same underlying problem: AI tools that technically work but haven't figured out what job people are actually hiring them for.

---

### Microsoft — Introducing the New Copilot with Home, Code, and Autopilot

**Source:** https://blogs.microsoft.com/blog/2026/09/25/introducing-the-new-copilot-with-home-code-and-autopilot/
**Credibility:** High (first-party announcement from Microsoft blog)

**What happened:** Microsoft announced what they're calling their biggest Copilot update to date, restructuring the product around three distinct modes: Home, Code, and Autopilot. The framing is ambitious — they're positioning Copilot as "a new OS for work that spans every model, every form factor, and every task." This is a major repositioning from Copilot as an AI assistant embedded in Office apps to Copilot as the primary work surface itself.

**Key capabilities:**
- **Home** — appears to be a personalized dashboard or starting point for AI-assisted work, likely replacing the existing Copilot home experience
- **Code** — dedicated coding mode, putting GitHub Copilot's capabilities more directly into the consumer Copilot product
- **Autopilot** — the most significant addition; an autonomous mode that can execute multi-step tasks without constant user input, signaling Microsoft's push into agentic workflows

**Why it matters for PMs:** This is the most direct signal yet that Microsoft sees Copilot not as a feature inside existing products but as the product itself. The three-mode structure is a classic PM move: segment by job-to-be-done (communicate/organize, build, automate) and give each its own experience rather than forcing everything through one UI. GitHub Copilot adding "When chat is the wrong UI" to their blog this same week is not a coincidence — there's a deliberate push happening across Microsoft to move past chat as the default interaction model for AI.

**Critical questions:**
- How does Autopilot handle errors and edge cases — and what does recovery look like when it goes wrong? This is the trust question that determines whether users actually delegate to it.
- What's the upgrade path for existing enterprise Copilot M365 customers? Does this cannibalize those SKUs or complement them?
- "New OS for work" is a big claim — what does Microsoft mean when they say "every model"? Are they opening to non-Azure models, or is this just framing?
- The three-mode structure assumes users know which mode they need. That's a real UX bet. How does onboarding work for someone who just wants help with email?

**Action you could take today:** If your product sits inside Microsoft's ecosystem (Slack, Figma, your own app), map out where Autopilot could either automate handoffs to your product or route users away from it entirely. That's the agentic disruption surface to watch.

### Julie Zhuo — What Will Make Personal AI Go Big?

**Source:** https://joulee.medium.com/what-will-make-personal-ai-go-big-e92fbb10aba9
**Credibility:** High (direct post from Julie Zhuo, former VP of Design at Facebook, now a respected product voice)

**What happened:** Julie Zhuo published a piece asking why personal AI hasn't crossed into mainstream adoption yet — and more importantly, what would actually move the needle. Based on the title and her usual framing, she's likely examining the gap between AI tools that technically capable early adopters use and the broad consumer market that hasn't found a reason to make AI a daily habit.

**Key patterns to note:** Zhuo consistently brings a design and human-centered lens to product questions that most AI coverage misses. Her question "what will make personal AI go big" is exactly the right PM question right now — the industry has largely been asking "how do we make AI more capable" when the adoption ceiling seems to be about fit, not capability.

**Why it matters for PMs:** The personal AI adoption question is one of the most important open questions in the space right now. We have extraordinarily capable models, but the retention patterns for AI features haven't matched the hype. If Zhuo's argument is that personal AI needs a clearer job-to-be-done — something people reach for automatically, not occasionally — that's directly relevant to anyone building consumer AI features. The question isn't "can AI do this" anymore. It's "will people build a habit around it?"

**Critical questions:**
- What's the difference between personal AI that users adopt deeply versus tools they try and abandon? Is it capability, workflow fit, or something about identity and trust?
- If personal AI goes big, what does the "killer use case" look like — and does it come from AI-native products or from AI embedded in existing habits (messaging, calendar, browser)?
- Does Zhuo's framing apply differently to enterprise vs. consumer contexts, or is the adoption gap the same in both?

**Action you could take today:** Pull your own product's AI feature retention data and compare it to non-AI feature retention at the same cohort stage. If AI features have higher initial activation but lower 30-day retention, you have exactly the problem Zhuo is describing.

### LangSmith — New Platform Release: Engine v2, Managed Deep Agents, Fine-Tuning, Trajectories

**Source:** https://www.langchain.com/blog/langsmith-engine-agents-fine-tuning-trajectories
**Credibility:** High (first-party announcement from LangChain)

**What happened:** LangSmith shipped a substantial platform update covering four distinct capabilities at once. Engine v2 adds red teaming and automated testing to the existing evaluation infrastructure. Managed Deep Agents 0.8 adds user-owned credentials, user-level memory, HTTP channels, Slack file transfer, and a built-in web search tool. LangSmith Fine-Tuning and a new CLI called SmithTune let teams train specialized models directly from LangSmith data without building custom pipelines. And Trajectories gives a conversational, readable view of every agent session to speed up debugging.

**Key technical details:**
- **Engine v2 red teaming**: proactively detects agent issues before users encounter them — this is the "test in production" problem solved at the platform level
- **SmithTune CLI**: post-training models from trace data without building a custom data pipeline, which lowers the bar for fine-tuning significantly
- **Trajectories**: converts raw trace data into a readable conversation flow — specifically designed for long-running agents where debugging a trace currently requires hours of manual review
- **Managed Deep Agents 0.8**: user-level memory (not just org-level) and HTTP channels are meaningful UX improvements for anyone building customer-facing agents

**Why it matters for PMs:** LangSmith is becoming less of an observability tool and more of a full-stack agent development platform. Fine-tuning from your own trace data, red teaming built in, and conversational debugging — this changes the economics of building specialized agents. PMs who previously thought fine-tuning required a separate ML infra investment should revisit that assumption. More importantly: the Trajectories feature addresses a very real pain point in agent development. When something goes wrong in a multi-step agent task, the debugging experience today is brutal. Making that readable is a genuine productivity unlock.

**Critical questions:**
- Fine-tuning from LangSmith trace data is appealing, but what's the data quality bar? If your traces include bad agent behavior, you're training on that too. What guardrails does SmithTune have?
- Managed Deep Agents with "user-owned credentials" — how does that work in enterprise contexts where credential management is a compliance concern?
- Red teaming in Engine v2 sounds great, but what does it actually probe for? Prompt injection? Task boundary violations? The scope matters a lot for how much security value this actually provides.

**Action you could take today:** If you're running agents in production with LangSmith, look at your longest and most-errored traces. Trajectories was built specifically for that pain. Check if the 0.8 release is available in your tier and run one of your problematic sessions through it.

GitHub is publishing "when chat is the wrong UI." Julie Zhuo is asking why personal AI hasn't gone mass market. ## Sit With This

Microsoft just rebuilt Copilot around three distinct modes — Home, Code, and Autopilot — explicitly because one chat interface wasn't working for all jobs. They gave each use case its own experience rather than forcing everything through a single UI.

**For your product:** If you have an AI feature that users try once but don't return to, is the problem the capability — or is it that you shipped one UI for three different jobs? What would it look like to give each job-to-be-done its own entry point?
