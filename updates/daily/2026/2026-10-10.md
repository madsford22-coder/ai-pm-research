# Daily PM Research Update: 2026-10-10

## The Short Version

Noah Shinn is rebuilding Instinct's "boring" core use cases (flights, hotels, customer service calls) even as the company chases flashier autonomy wins, while LangChain quietly published the exact kind of result that justifies an agent harness investment: a 64% cost cut with no quality loss on real coding tasks.

### Instinct - Rebuilding the Flight and Hotel Experience Instead of Chasing New Autonomy Wins
**Source:** https://x.com/noahrshinn (multiple posts, Oct 7 & Oct 10, 2026)
**Credibility:** High (founder, first-party product commentary)

**What happened:** Noah Shinn posted a short series on Instinct's product priorities this week. The headline number: more than 20% of Instinct's users had the agent help with flights last month, and the team just rebuilt that flight experience from scratch. On the same day, Shinn also posted about extending "Instinct Concierge" so the agent can make customer service calls on the user's behalf, going beyond the original scope of simply handling the call. Then, this morning, he announced a new hotel booking experience built around a specific philosophy: "You shouldn't have to work around someone else's app or website to get something done. The interface should be built for you, every time."

**Key capabilities:**
- Flight booking/assistance used by over 20% of Instinct's user base in the last month — the clearest usage number Instinct has shared publicly
- Instinct Concierge now extends to making calls on the user's behalf for customer service tasks, not just navigating menus
- New hotel booking experience, explicitly designed to avoid routing users through third-party apps/websites
- Pattern across all three posts: Instinct is choosing to go deep on its "boring," high-frequency use cases rather than only expanding into novel categories

**Why it matters for PMs:** This is the tension every agent product eventually hits: the sexy pitch is "autonomous AI that does anything," but the usage data pulls you back to a handful of unglamorous, high-frequency jobs. Shinn is explicitly naming that tradeoff ("we spend a lot of time on things that go well beyond classic use cases, but the classic use cases still have to work"). The 20% flight-usage number is also one of the only concrete adoption stats we've seen from Instinct, and it's a signal for the open question about how much autonomy and delegated action users actually want from a personal agent: they want it to handle logistics, not have philosophical reach.

**Critical questions:**
- Is 20% of users touching flight booking a strong signal of retention, or a one-time novelty action (most people don't book flights weekly)?
- What does "rebuilt from scratch" actually mean here — was the original flight flow broken, or is this positioning a UX problem as a technical one?
- If Instinct Concierge is now making calls autonomously, what's the error-recovery path when it books the wrong flight or misstates something to a human agent on a support call?
- Does building a custom interface for every vertical (flights, hotels, customer service) scale, or does Instinct end up re-implementing travel-booking UX that Expedia/Kayak already solved?

**Action you could take today:** If you're building an agent product, pull your own usage data and check: are users concentrating on 2-3 "boring" jobs-to-be-done, or spreading thin across many features? That ratio should drive your next quarter's roadmap more than your competitive positioning does.

### LangChain - Cut Median Coding Task Cost by 64% With a Model Router, No Quality Loss
**Source:** https://www.langchain.com/blog/how-to-build-a-model-router-in-the-harness
**Credibility:** High (first-party engineering writeup with methodology and results)

**What happened:** LangChain's team built a model router into Open SWE's agent harness — the layer that decides which model handles which step of a coding task — and used it to cut the median cost per coding task by 64%, with "no measurable drop in quality." They've published how they built it, positioned explicitly as a template other teams can replicate.

**Key technical details:**
- Model router sits in the harness layer (not the agent prompt layer), routing individual steps of a coding task to different models based on task complexity
- Result: 64% reduction in median cost per task
- Claim: no measurable quality degradation from the routing strategy
- Framed as a reusable pattern, not a one-off optimization specific to Open SWE

**Why it matters for PMs:** Every team running agentic coding or support workflows at any volume eventually hits a model-cost wall, and the obvious move (use one expensive frontier model for everything) is the most wasteful option, not the safest one. This is a concrete, measured answer to the build-vs-buy and cost-vs-capability questions PMs keep circling: you don't need to downgrade quality to control cost, you need a routing layer that matches model capability to task difficulty step by step. It's also a direct, publishable number you can bring into a budget conversation instead of "we think agents are getting cheaper."

**Critical questions:**
- What's the actual task mix being routed — is "no quality loss" measured against a narrow set of coding tasks, or does it hold across edge cases and harder refactors?
- How much engineering investment did the router itself take to build, and does that investment pay for itself below a certain usage volume?
- Does this pattern hold outside of coding agents — classification/routing is easier when you can grade "did the code work," harder in open-ended tasks like writing or research synthesis
- What's the latency cost of routing decisions themselves — does the router add meaningful overhead per step?

**Action you could take today:** If your team runs agent workflows on a single frontier model by default, audit your task logs for a day: segment by task complexity and estimate what percentage could run on a cheaper model without quality loss. That's your business case for building (or buying) a router.

### Simon Willison - Shipped a Voice-Driven Feature for His Own Blog
**Source:** https://simonwillison.net/2026/Oct/9/built-using-my-voice/
**Credibility:** High (first-party build log from a widely-followed independent practitioner)

**What happened:** Simon Willison built and shipped a new feature for his blog using voice as the primary input/interaction method, documenting the process as a build log. This continues his pattern of publishing hands-on, reproducible experiments with AI-assisted development rather than commentary from the sidelines.

**Key capabilities:**
- A real, shipped feature on a production blog (not a toy demo), built primarily through voice interaction with an AI tool
- Full build log published for others to reproduce the approach
- Part of a broader personal practice of treating his own blog as a testbed for new AI-assisted workflows

**Why it matters for PMs:** Willison's blog build logs are consistently some of the most concrete, unspun evidence available on what AI-assisted development actually feels like day to day, which matters more than vendor benchmarks when you're deciding whether to invest in voice-driven coding workflows (Wispr Flow, voice-to-code tooling, etc.) for your own team. When a practitioner this skeptical and detail-oriented ships something real with a given workflow, that's a stronger signal than a product launch post.

**Critical questions:**
- How much of this was genuinely voice-first versus voice-assisted with heavy manual cleanup afterward?
- Would this workflow hold up for a more complex feature than a personal blog, where correctness and regression risk are higher?
- What's the actual time comparison versus his typical text-prompt-driven workflow?

**Action you could take today:** Read the build log and compare the friction points he documents against your own team's current AI-coding workflow; it's a quick gut check on whether voice input is worth piloting versus staying text-first.
