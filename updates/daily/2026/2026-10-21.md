# Daily PM Research Update: 2026-10-21

## The Short Version

Notion rebuilt its AI pricing around a credit system instead of flat-rate access, OpenAI is quietly becoming the ad platform and the agentic model router all at once, and Marty Cagan is making the case that AI breaks the entire premise of "ship fast and see what sticks" experimentation culture.

## Notion - Agents, Rebuilt to Do More for Less (New Credit-Based Pricing)

**Source:** https://www.notion.com/blog/notion-ai-more-for-less
**Credibility:** High (first-party product announcement from co-founder Akshay Kotharia, paired with companion engineering post)

**What happened:** Notion rebuilt Notion AI around a new credit system, replacing flat usage with metered consumption tied to a smarter model router. The companion engineering post ("Inside Notion's upgraded model router") explains the mechanics: pairwise comparisons and calibrated judging let Notion measure model quality at scale and route requests to the cheapest model that can still do the job. Notion is also adding bring-your-own-AI options, meaning customers can plug in their own model/API keys instead of consuming Notion's credits at all.

**Key technical details:**
- New model router makes per-request decisions using "pairwise comparisons and calibrated judging" to measure model quality rather than routing on static rules
- Long-running agents are now supported as a first-class capability, not just single-turn Q&A
- Bring-your-own-AI option lets enterprises use their own model contracts/keys
- Credit system replaces whatever flat-rate or seat-based AI access existed before
- Shipped alongside a separate post on connecting Notion AI to 160 new apps via an internal Notion agent (their dogfooding story)

**Why it matters for PMs:** This is the tension every PM shipping LLM features eventually hits: flat pricing is easy to sell but impossible to sustain once usage patterns diverge (a power user running long agent loops costs 100x a casual Q&A user). Credit systems solve the unit economics problem but introduce a new UX problem, now users have to think about consumption, which is exactly the kind of friction that kills adoption of "invisible" AI features. Notion's bet is that bring-your-own-AI plus a smarter router (cheaper models when quality allows) buys them enough margin that they don't have to make the credit meter feel punitive. Worth watching whether this becomes the new default pattern, the way seat-based SaaS pricing became default a decade ago.

**Critical questions:**
- How transparent is Notion making the credit cost of a given action before a user triggers it? Surprise consumption is the fastest way to erode trust in a metered system.
- Does "calibrated judging" mean Notion is running its own eval suite per request, and if so, what's the latency cost of that overhead?
- Will bring-your-own-AI cannibalize Notion's own AI revenue from the power users who'd pay the most, or is it aimed at a different (cost-sensitive, high-volume) segment?

**Action you could take today:** If your product has flat-rate AI pricing, pull your last 30 days of per-user inference cost data and check the distribution, if your top 5% of users cost more than 20x your median user, you have the same problem Notion just tried to solve.

## OpenAI - ChatGPT Ads Expands to 26 Countries, Plus the Ad Stack Engineering Post

**Source:** https://openai.com/index/chatgpt-ads-expands-26-new-countries and https://openai.com/index/building-chatgpt-ad-stack
**Credibility:** High (first-party product and engineering posts, same-day pairing)

**What happened:** OpenAI expanded ChatGPT Ads to 26 new countries, with over 100,000 advertisers now on the platform. In a companion engineering post, OpenAI detailed how it built the ad stack to keep ads separate from conversational answers while still using "conversational intent" to target relevance. Same week, OpenAI also announced its models are coming to Google Cloud's Model Garden, putting Codex workflows and GPT models directly inside Gemini's enterprise distribution channel.

**Key technical details:**
- Ad stack is described as "LLM-powered," using conversational intent signals rather than traditional keyword/behavioral targeting
- Explicit architectural separation between ad serving and answer generation, OpenAI is framing this as a trust decision, not just an engineering one
- 100,000+ advertisers onboarded before this international expansion
- Separately: OpenAI models (GPT-5.6, GPT-6, GPT-6.1 per the AWS Bedrock Canada listing elsewhere this week) are now distributed through competitors' marketplaces (AWS Bedrock, and now Google Cloud Model Garden)

**Why it matters for PMs:** The ad stack post matters more than the expansion number. OpenAI is publishing its reasoning for *why* ads and answers are architecturally separated, this is the same trust problem every PM building AI recommendations or AI search has to solve: how do you monetize without users suspecting the model is shilling for a paying advertiser. The multi-cloud distribution move (Bedrock, Google Cloud) is the more interesting business signal: OpenAI is choosing distribution over exclusivity, which tells you model access is becoming commoditized faster than the "GPT moat" narrative suggests.

**Critical questions:**
- Does "conversational intent" targeting mean OpenAI is reading further into user conversations than people assume when they see "sponsored" labels? What's actually disclosed to users?
- If OpenAI models are available on AWS Bedrock *and* Google Cloud, what's left of OpenAI's distribution moat versus Anthropic's or Google's own models?
- Is 100,000 advertisers a meaningful base rate, or inflated by low-friction self-serve signups with minimal spend?

**Action you could take today:** If you're building any AI feature with a monetization layer (ads, affiliate, sponsored results), read OpenAI's ad stack post as a reference architecture for how to structurally separate paid and organic outputs, then audit whether your own product has that same hard boundary or a soft one.

## Marty Cagan - Experimentation, AI and the Product Model

**Source:** https://www.svpg.com/experimentation-ai-and-the-product-model/
**Credibility:** High (recognized PM authority, SVPG, direct product strategy commentary)

**What happened:** Cagan argues that AI is breaking a core assumption behind modern product experimentation culture: that you can cheaply test many ideas and let data decide. His point is that AI-assisted development makes it cheap to *build* variants, but the real cost of experimentation was never the build, it was the organizational cost of evaluating, deciding, and committing to outcomes. He's pushing back on the idea that "AI means more experiments" is automatically good product practice.

**Key patterns:**
- Separates "build cost" (which AI is collapsing) from "decision cost" (which AI does nothing for, and may be making worse by generating more variants to evaluate)
- Warns against mistaking higher experiment *volume* for better experiment *quality* or faster learning
- Reiterates the product model's dependency on teams being outcome-accountable, not just output-prolific, a recurring SVPG theme that's now colliding directly with AI-era velocity claims
- Frames this as a leadership and process problem, not a tooling problem: the bottleneck moves from "can we build it" to "can we decide what matters"

**Why it matters for PMs:** This is the sharpest pushback this week against the "AI makes PM faster" narrative, and it's worth taking seriously because Cagan isn't anti-AI, he's anti-sloppy-process. If your team adopted AI coding tools and your shipping velocity went up but your win rate on experiments didn't, this is why: you've optimized the part of the funnel that was never actually the bottleneck. This directly connects to Ravi Mehta's point this week (Product Delivery, Oct 15) that AI expands engineering capacity but the job is still building the right thing well, not just building more things.

**Critical questions:**
- Is there evidence yet that teams running more AI-assisted experiments are seeing better hit rates, or just more experiments with the same hit rate (meaning more noise, not more signal)?
- What does "decision cost" actually look like operationally, is it stakeholder alignment time, QA/validation time, or something else Cagan isn't naming directly?
- How does this interact with eval-driven development (the Terminal X and other OpenAI customer posts this week show companies building rubric-based evals), which is explicitly trying to industrialize the decision step?

**Action you could take today:** Count how many experiments your team shipped last quarter versus how many you actually made a clear ship/kill decision on within two weeks of results, if there's a big gap, that's your decision bottleneck made visible.

## The Thread

**The build/decide split is this week's real pattern.** Cagan's experimentation post, Ravi Mehta's "Product Delivery" piece from earlier in the week, and Notion's own agent-building post (connecting 160 apps "the secret was a Notion agent") are all converging on the same idea: AI has made building cheap and fast, but the actual PM job, deciding what's worth building and whether it worked, hasn't gotten any easier. If anything, cheap building is making the decision bottleneck more visible, not less important.
