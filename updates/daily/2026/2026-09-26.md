# Daily PM Research Update: 2026-09-26

## The Short Version

A quiet Friday with a sharp question underneath: as AI tools keep accelerating output, Karri Saarinen asks whether teams are actually learning more — and Microsoft's new Copilot redesign makes you wonder the same thing about AI assistants built for scale.

---

### Karri Saarinen — Tools Are Getting Better. Are We?
**Source:** https://x.com/karrisaarinen/status/2103512094981419349
**Credibility:** Medium (first-person reflection from a founder and design leader with direct experience shipping AI tools)

**What happened:** Karri Saarinen, Linear's co-founder and CEO, posted a short but pointed observation on September 25: AI and agents are producing more code and prototypes, but the focus has shifted entirely to output — and he's questioning whether teams are actually developing better judgment and understanding as a result. The concern isn't about productivity. It's about whether the teams using these tools are getting sharper or just faster.

**Key craft patterns:**
- Volume of output has decoupled from depth of learning — more PRs, prototypes, and iterations don't automatically mean better product thinking
- The tooling optimizes for completion, not comprehension — agents finish tasks, but the human doesn't necessarily understand what was built or why it works
- "Output over understanding" is a real risk in teams adopting agentic workflows at speed

**Why it matters for PMs:** This is a question every PM running a team with AI coding tools should be sitting with. If developers are merging AI-generated code they don't fully understand, your technical debt problem is about to get quieter and harder to detect. The same applies to PM workflows — if you're using AI to generate research summaries, write specs, or draft strategies you haven't thought through, you may be producing more while learning less. Saarinen is pointing at an organizational drift that's easy to miss because all the surface metrics look good.

**Critical questions:**
- How do you measure whether your team is developing judgment, not just throughput? Are there leading indicators you can track?
- If a developer can't explain an AI-generated change in a PR review, does that get flagged? Should it?
- Is the answer to slow down AI tool adoption, or to redesign the human review process around it?
- What does "understanding" even mean for AI-assisted work — does it require being able to reproduce it, or just being able to evaluate it?

**Action you could take today:** In your next sprint review or retro, add one question to the conversation: "What did we learn this sprint that we didn't know before?" If the team struggles to answer it, that's a signal worth taking seriously.

### Microsoft Copilot — A New Architecture for Work
**Source:** https://blogs.microsoft.com/blog/2026/09/25/introducing-the-new-copilot-with-home-code-and-autopilot/
**Credibility:** High (first-party announcement from Microsoft)

**What happened:** Microsoft shipped its biggest Copilot redesign to date on September 25. The update positions Copilot as "a new OS for work" with three major additions: Home (a personalized dashboard that surfaces tasks and context across apps), Code (integrated coding assistance that spans VS Code and Microsoft 365), and Autopilot (an autonomous mode that can take multi-step actions without constant user prompting). This is available across models and form factors.

**Key capabilities:**
- **Home**: A new landing experience that aggregates tasks, meetings, documents, and priority work from across Microsoft 365 — think of it as a persistent awareness layer rather than a search bar
- **Code**: Copilot now works across the boundary between Office and VS Code, letting a developer in one environment reference or act on content from the other — this is a meaningful workflow integration, not just feature parity
- **Autopilot**: Multi-step autonomous action mode that can complete longer tasks with less back-and-forth; this is Microsoft's direct answer to agentic interfaces from competitors

**Why it matters for PMs:** Three things to pay attention to here. First, the "OS for work" framing is a deliberate category claim — Microsoft is no longer positioning Copilot as a feature inside apps, it's positioning it as the surface above apps. That's a significant strategic shift. Second, Autopilot's autonomous mode is Microsoft's bet that enterprise users are ready to delegate multi-step tasks to AI — a real signal on the autonomy question that's been live for the past year. Third, the Home layer is the most interesting thing in this launch: a persistent, personalized context surface is a different product category than a chatbot. Watch whether users actually use it as a home base or ignore it.

**Critical questions:**
- Enterprise buyers adopted Copilot at the feature level. Does reframing it as an "OS for work" create friction or accelerate broader adoption?
- Autopilot requires users to trust AI with multi-step actions in high-stakes environments. What's the rollback and error-recovery story when something goes wrong?
- Home as a persistent dashboard assumes Copilot has enough reliable context to be useful — what happens when it surfaces wrong or stale information and users lose trust?
- How does this change the Copilot pricing story? If it's now an OS, should it be priced as infrastructure or as a per-seat feature?

**Action you could take today:** If you're shipping a product inside an enterprise that touches Microsoft 365, spend 20 minutes mapping where Copilot's new Home layer might surface your product's data — or where it might compete with your product's existing "hub" function.

### Shreya Shankar — The Evals Dialectic
**Source:** https://x.com/sh_reya
**Credibility:** High (Shreya Shankar is a researcher and practitioner with deep technical expertise in ML systems and evals)

**What happened:** On September 23, Shreya Shankar posted a follow-up to her earlier evals work: "The uncomfortable dialectic is that (1) evals are necessary to build good agents, but also (2) agents are extremely helpful (and necessary) for automating tedious parts of the evals lifecycle." This is a compact statement of a real tension that's emerging in teams building agentic systems — and it echoes what she's been documenting for months.

**Key patterns:**
- Evals and agents are now mutually dependent: you need evals to build reliable agents, and agents to build reliable evals at scale
- This creates a bootstrapping problem — early-stage teams often skip evals because they're expensive to build, which means they can't tell if their agents work
- The community resonance she mentions suggests this pattern is widely recognized but not yet widely solved

**Why it matters for PMs:** If you're on a team shipping agents, the "we'll add evals later" decision is increasingly risky. The teams that get ahead here will be the ones that use agents to build their eval infrastructure early, creating a compounding advantage. If your team hasn't mapped the evals question yet, this is a concrete framing to bring to an eng conversation — not "should we have evals" but "which parts of building evals can we automate with agents right now."

**Critical questions:**
- What's the minimum viable eval setup for a team that's just starting on agents? Where do you start when you can't afford to build everything?
- If agents generate evals, who validates the validators? What does human oversight look like in this loop?
- How do you know when your evals are actually measuring the right things, versus measuring what's easy to measure?

**Action you could take today:** Ask your engineering team: "What would it take to have a basic automated eval running on our agent outputs within two weeks?" If nobody has an answer, that gap is worth closing now before the agent surface area grows.

## The Thread

**The output-versus-understanding gap is showing up everywhere.** Karri Saarinen named it directly this week, but you can see the same tension in Shreya Shankar's evals dialectic (agents produce more but you need infrastructure to know if it's good), in Microsoft's Autopilot bet (enterprise users are being handed more autonomy before we've solved error recovery), and in the broader pattern of AI tools optimizing for throughput. The question for product teams isn't whether to use these tools — it's how to maintain the judgment infrastructure alongside the output infrastructure.

## Sit With This

Karri Saarinen's observation cuts directly at something that's easy to rationalize away: your team is shipping more, reviews look clean, velocity is up — but nobody can explain why a key architectural decision was made last sprint because an agent made it and the PR was approved on signal strength alone.

**For your team:** Pick one AI-assisted decision your team made in the last two weeks — a spec, an architectural choice, a prioritization call — and ask yourself: could we reconstruct the reasoning from scratch if we had to? If not, what does that mean for your ability to course-correct when something goes wrong?
