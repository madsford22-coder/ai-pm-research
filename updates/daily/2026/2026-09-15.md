# Daily PM Research Update: 2026-09-15

### Maggie Appleton - Planning with Agents: Divided Worlds, Boundary Objects, and Thicker Interfaces

**Source:** https://maggieappleton.com/planning-agents/
**Credibility:** High (original design research essay from a respected practitioner and design engineer with a track record of rigorous thinking on human-computer interaction)

**What happened:** Maggie Appleton published a substantial essay arguing that the current generation of AI planning agents is failing not because of model capability, but because of interface design. She introduces the concept of "boundary objects" — shared representations that let humans and agents build a common understanding of a planning space — and argues that most agent UIs are too thin: they show a chat window and a final output, but nothing of the reasoning, constraints, or tradeoffs in between. The result is that humans can't meaningfully collaborate with agents on complex planning tasks; they can only accept or reject outputs.

**Key design and product patterns:**
- "Divided worlds" framing: humans and agents operate in separate epistemic spaces with no shared artifact connecting them during the planning process
- Boundary objects need to be legible to both parties — the agent needs to be able to read and update them, and the human needs to understand them without a CS degree
- Thicker interfaces would expose intermediate states: partial plans, constraint lists, dependency graphs, confidence levels, branching alternatives
- Current chat-based interfaces collapse the entire reasoning process into a single exchange, which destroys collaborative potential
- Historical parallels: whiteboard diagrams, Gantt charts, kanban boards all function as boundary objects in human-human planning; we don't have equivalents for human-agent planning

**Why it matters for PMs:** If you're building any product that involves agents doing planning work — trip planning, project management, scheduling, research synthesis — this essay reframes your core UX problem. The question isn't "what can the agent do?" It's "what artifact can the human and agent both hold, modify, and point at together?" Most current products aren't even asking this question. The teams that figure it out first will build the tools people actually trust to handle complex, stakes-bearing decisions.

**Critical questions:**
- How do you design a boundary object that's rich enough to support genuine collaboration but not so complex it becomes a second job to maintain?
- Which planning domains are most tractable right now, given model limitations on maintaining consistent constraint sets across long sessions?
- Is there a risk that thicker interfaces paradoxically reduce trust, because they expose how much uncertainty the agent is actually carrying?
- What does this mean for the chat-first product patterns that most AI tool teams defaulted to in 2024-2025?

**Action you could take today:** Pull up your current agent or AI-assisted workflow product and identify: what artifact exists that both the user and the AI can see and modify simultaneously? If the answer is "just the chat thread," that's the gap Appleton is pointing at. Draft one alternative interface concept that surfaces intermediate state.

### Thomas Wolf / Hugging Face x IBM Research - Your Agent Aced the Task. Will It Do It Again?

**Source:** https://huggingface.co/blog/ibm-research/altk-evolve-consistency
**Credibility:** Medium-High (IBM Research post surfaced and amplified by Thomas Wolf at Hugging Face; describes a published evaluation framework with methodology, though full paper details aren't in the excerpt)

**What happened:** IBM Research published work on agent task consistency — the problem that an agent can complete a task successfully once, but that doesn't mean it will do it reliably across runs. The piece introduces ALTK-Evolve, a framework for evaluating agent consistency specifically, rather than just task success rate. The core finding: pass rate on a benchmark is a poor proxy for production reliability. An agent that scores 80% might achieve that by getting different tasks right on different runs, rather than being consistently reliable on any given task.

**Key technical details:**
- ALTK-Evolve is designed to measure whether agent success is reproducible, not just achievable
- The framework runs agents across multiple trials on the same tasks and scores for consistency across runs, not just average pass rate
- Distinction between "ever succeeds" and "reliably succeeds" — the gap between these is where production deployments break down
- Consistency scores can be computed at the task level, revealing which task types the agent handles dependably vs. which it flukes through
- The framework is designed to be model-agnostic and applicable across agent types

**Why it matters for PMs:** This is the eval conversation that most product teams haven't had yet. When you're scoping an agent feature, your engineering team will show you benchmark numbers. Those numbers tell you what the agent can do on a good day. What ALTK-Evolve reveals is whether the agent is actually trustworthy enough to promise to users. The gap between "sometimes works" and "reliably works" is exactly the gap that determines whether an agent feature can ship as a core product experience or has to live behind a disclaimer. This framework gives you a principled way to ask: what's our consistency target, and are we there?

**Critical questions:**
- What consistency threshold is actually acceptable for different product contexts? (High-stakes task completion vs. low-stakes suggestion are very different bars)
- How do you communicate agent consistency to users without undermining their confidence in the product?
- Does measuring consistency across runs actually capture the failure modes users care about, or does it miss context-dependent failures?
- How much does consistency vary by model, task type, and prompt structure — and which lever is most effective to pull?

**Action you could take today:** Look at your agent evals (or ask your team to pull them). Are you measuring pass rate or consistency rate? If you're only measuring pass rate, you don't yet know if your agent is production-ready — you know if it's demo-ready. That's a different thing.

### Stripe - What Stripe Data Shows About Fraud at AI Startups

**Source:** https://stripe.com/blog/industry
**Credibility:** High (first-party data from Stripe's infrastructure; written by Jacob Meltzer, Engineering Lead at Stripe Radar, and Samuel Bakouch, Head of Product for Payments Intelligence — this is not a marketing piece)

**What happened:** Stripe published a data-driven analysis of fraud patterns specifically at AI startups, drawing on their payments infrastructure data. The piece is written by the team that actually runs fraud detection at Stripe — this is operational intelligence, not thought leadership. Based on the excerpt, the post analyzes what fraud looks like at AI companies, which likely includes patterns around API abuse, account takeover, credit card fraud on subscription checkouts, and free tier exploitation.

**Key patterns (based on excerpt and title context):**
- Stripe Radar data gives them a cross-industry view of fraud that individual companies can't replicate from their own data alone
- AI startups likely face distinct fraud patterns: free tier abuse to mine model credits, synthetic identity fraud, and bulk account creation to exploit trial periods
- The post separates AI startup fraud from general SaaS fraud, which suggests the patterns are meaningfully different
- Written by both an engineering lead and a product lead — this is a signal that fraud at AI companies is now a product-level concern, not just an ops problem

**Why it matters for PMs:** If you're building an AI product with any kind of free tier, trial period, or usage-based pricing, this is required reading. The fraud patterns unique to AI startups — credit farming, model output reselling, synthetic account creation — are different from traditional SaaS fraud, and your risk team may not have a playbook for them yet. Stripe is essentially publishing the threat model for your product's business layer. Understanding it helps you design pricing, access controls, and abuse detection before you get burned.

**Critical questions:**
- Which fraud patterns are specific to AI products vs. which are just amplified versions of general SaaS fraud?
- At what scale do these fraud patterns typically emerge — day one, or after you hit a certain user or revenue threshold?
- How do you balance friction (to prevent fraud) against conversion (to grow the product) in your onboarding flow?
- What signals should a PM watch for in their own data that might indicate fraud is eating into usage metrics?

**Action you could take today:** Read the full Stripe post and then walk through your product's free tier or trial structure asking: where could a bad actor extract value here, and what would it look like in our usage data? Write down three specific signals you'd want to monitor.

## The Thread

**The gap between "it worked" and "you can count on it" is the defining PM problem in agents right now.** Maggie Appleton's essay on interface design, Thomas Wolf's consistency evaluation framework, and even Stripe's fraud data all point at the same underlying issue: agents can perform, but we don't yet have the infrastructure — interfaces, evals, trust signals, or abuse controls — that would let us promise users they'll perform reliably. The teams making real progress aren't the ones with the most capable models. They're the ones who've figured out how to make capability visible, repeatable, and trustworthy. That's a product and design problem, not a model problem.

## Sit With This

Maggie Appleton's essay argues that agent interfaces fail at collaborative planning because there's no shared artifact — no "boundary object" — that both the human and the AI can see and modify together. The chat thread collapses all the reasoning into a single exchange, making genuine back-and-forth on complex decisions nearly impossible.

**For your product:** What's the boundary object in your current agent or AI feature? If your answer is "the chat history" or "the final output," what would it take to expose the intermediate state — the constraints, the tradeoffs, the branching possibilities — in a way that your users could actually engage with? And is there a version of that which makes the product *more* trustworthy rather than more overwhelming?
