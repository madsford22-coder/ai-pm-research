# Daily PM Research Update: 2026-10-04

## The Short Version

Three different companies are converging on the same hard problem this week (how much access and autonomy a personal AI agent should get) while Notion quietly ships the infrastructure layer (agent skills, shared memory) that makes any of this usable at scale.

### OpenAI / Lenny Rachitsky - Why Dots Is OpenAI's Biggest Bet, According to Its Own Head of ChatGPT
**Source:** https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/openais-head-of-chatgpt-were-entering
**Credibility:** High (direct interview with Tibo Sottiaux, OpenAI's Head of ChatGPT, on Lenny's podcast)

**What happened:** Lenny interviewed Tibo Sottiaux, who runs ChatGPT at OpenAI, about why Dots (OpenAI's agentic product, launched at DevDay) is the company's biggest internal bet right now. Sottiaux's argument: agents are going to account for a growing share of internet traffic, and the builders who are still treating this like "chatbot plus plugins" are missing where the puck is going.

**Key capabilities:**
- Sottiaux frames Dots as a bet on agents replacing a meaningful share of how people interact with the web, not just a chat feature bolt-on
- The interview digs into what builders are "still getting wrong" when designing for agent-first usage, specifically around handing off tasks vs. keeping humans in a chat loop
- This comes right after OpenAI's DevDay, where Dots was one of 20+ launches but positioned internally as the headline bet, per Sottiaux

**Why it matters for PMs:** This is a rare moment where you get the internal rationale straight from the person running the product, not a press release. If OpenAI's own leadership is explicitly calling Dots (not GPT-6, not Sol/Luna) the biggest bet, that's a signal about where OpenAI thinks the margin and distribution fight actually is: agentic task completion, not model quality. For anyone building a product that touches ChatGPT (plugins, GPTs, API integrations), this is a signal to re-evaluate whether your integration still works in an agent-first world, or whether it was built for a chat-first one that's already fading.

**Critical questions:**
- "Agents will dominate internet traffic" is a bold claim. What's the actual usage data behind Dots today versus this being aspirational framing?
- If Dots succeeds, what happens to the long tail of GPTs and plugin-based integrations built for the old paradigm?
- Sottiaux is the product leader selling the bet internally and externally. What's the view from engineering or research on whether Dots is actually ready for this scale of traffic shift?

**Action you could take today:** If your product has any ChatGPT integration (plugin, Actions, API), spend 30 minutes auditing whether it's designed for a user typing a prompt or an agent executing a multi-step task autonomously. Those are different integration surfaces.

### Notion - A Skills Library and Shared Memory Layer for Agents
**Source:** https://www.notion.com/blog/a-skills-library-for-every-agent (referenced alongside https://www.notion.com/blog/building-shared-memory-for-ai-agents-in-notion)
**Credibility:** High (first-party product announcement with named engineering authors)

**What happened:** Notion shipped a skills library that lets teams manage agent skills using Notion's existing editing, collaboration, and governance model, then reuse those skills across different agents. This sits alongside Notion's "Lore" shared memory system, which turns conversations, decisions, and tasks into durable Notion pages that future agents and people can reference. Together they're building the infrastructure layer for agent operations inside a company, not just a single chatbot feature.

**Key technical details:**
- Skills are managed with the same editing, permissions, and collaboration model Notion already uses for pages, so there's no new governance system to learn
- Lore converts ephemeral agent context (conversations, decisions, tasks) into persistent, referenceable Notion pages
- Both features are explicitly framed as working "everywhere," i.e., across multiple agents, not locked to one assistant

**Why it matters for PMs:** This is the same pattern we flagged with Wispr Flow's team features: individual AI tools are easy to adopt, but what makes them sticky at the org level is shared infrastructure. Notion is building the plumbing (skills, memory, governance) that makes agents usable by a team instead of a single power user. If you're building an agent feature and ignoring the "how does a second person on the team inherit this context" problem, Notion just showed you what that infrastructure looks like.

**Critical questions:**
- Does reusing Notion's existing permission model actually work for agent skills, which may need more granular, action-level controls than a wiki page does?
- How does Lore handle conflicting or outdated decisions? Stale "shared memory" could be worse than no memory.
- Is this actually used internally at meaningful scale, or is it an early capability being marketed ahead of real adoption data?

**Action you could take today:** If your team is building multiple agents (support bot, internal copilot, etc.), check whether they're each maintaining separate, siloed context. If so, sketch what a shared "skills + memory" layer would need to look like for your stack.

### Ben Tossell - "My Dot Experience So Far Has Been 💩"
**Source:** https://x.com/bentossell/status/2105817498180608298
**Credibility:** Medium (single practitioner's hands-on critical take, not a controlled study)

**What happened:** Ben Tossell, a well-known builder and early AI tools curator, posted blunt criticism of his experience using OpenAI's Dots product, just days after Sottiaux publicly framed Dots as OpenAI's biggest bet.

**Why it matters for PMs:** This is exactly the kind of signal that's easy to miss if you only read company blog posts. OpenAI's own leadership is betting the house on Dots internally, and a respected early-adopter builder is publicly saying the real-world experience doesn't match the pitch yet. That gap between internal conviction and external usability is one of the most useful things a PM can track when a company makes a big agentic bet. Launch hype and lived user experience are two different data points, and you need both.

**Critical questions:**
- Is Tossell's complaint about core functionality (the agent fails at tasks) or about UX friction (hard to set up, confusing interface)? Those point to very different fixes.
- How representative is one power user's negative experience of the broader rollout?
- Will OpenAI treat this kind of public critical feedback as a leading indicator, or dismiss it as early-access noise?

**Action you could take today:** Next time you ship an agentic feature, find your most vocal skeptical power user and get their unfiltered reaction within 48 hours of launch, before the PR narrative sets in. That reaction is more useful than your NPS survey.

## The Thread

**Everyone's racing to solve the same trust problem from a different angle.** OpenAI's Dots is betting big on agent autonomy while a respected builder is publicly saying the lived experience isn't there yet. Nat Friedman is opening up Muse to third-party hardware, which only works if users trust the agent enough to let it control their smart home. Notion is building the shared-memory and skills infrastructure that lets a team trust an agent's context instead of starting from zero every time. And Dare Obasanjo's regulatory flag (humans must review AI firing recommendations) is the policy version of the exact same question: how much do we let the agent decide on its own? This is the autonomy question from our open-questions file playing out in real time across four completely different companies in the same week.
