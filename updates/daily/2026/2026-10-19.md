# Daily PM Research Update: 2026-10-19

## The Short Version

Notion just ripped out its AI pricing model and replaced it with a credit system that supports bring-your-own-model, while Karri Saarinen is publicly wrestling with the same problem from the builder's chair: when AI makes it trivial to add features, what stops your product from turning into an incoherent pile of additive junk.

### Notion - Rebuilds Notion AI Around Credits and Model Routing, Not Flat Seats
**Source:** https://www.notion.com/blog/notion-ai-more-for-less
**Credibility:** High (first-party product announcement from co-founder Akshay Kothari)

**What happened:** Notion rebuilt its AI product around a new credit-based system, smarter model routing, long-running agents, and the ability to bring your own AI (BYO model/API key) instead of being locked into Notion's bundled models. Co-founder Akshay Kothari frames it as "more for less" — the same or better AI capability at lower effective cost per task, driven by routing requests to the cheapest model that can handle them well rather than a single default model for everyone.

**Key technical details:**
- New credit system replaces (or supplements) the prior flat-inclusion model for AI usage
- Smarter model routing: requests get sent to different underlying models depending on task complexity, rather than one model for all AI calls
- Long-running agents: tasks can now execute over extended time horizons instead of single-shot completions
- Bring-your-own-AI: users/orgs can plug in their own model or API key instead of relying solely on Notion-provided models
- Ships alongside a related post on how Notion connected its AI to 160 new third-party apps in three weeks using an internal Notion agent to handle the Figma-style setup and testing work

**Why it matters for PMs:** This is the tension between "AI feature as included perk" and "AI feature as metered cost center" playing out in real time. Flat-rate AI inclusion was a land-grab tactic — it got AI into every workspace without forcing a purchasing conversation. A credit system means Notion now has granular cost attribution and can tie usage to willingness-to-pay, but it also means power users will start watching a meter, which changes behavior. The BYO-model option is the more interesting signal: it's an admission that no single model wins on cost/quality for every task, and that locking users into one model is a liability as the model market gets more competitive and more commoditized. If you're pricing an AI feature, this is the shape of where things are heading — usage-based credits plus the option to swap in your own model, instead of a single AI tier at a fixed price.

**Critical questions:**
- Does moving to credits reduce AI usage among price-sensitive users, undermining the habit-formation Notion spent two years building?
- How does "bring your own model" affect Notion's unit economics if their heaviest users opt out of Notion-provided models entirely?
- Model routing improves cost efficiency, but does it introduce inconsistency in output quality across similar tasks, and will users notice?
- What happens to the simplicity of Notion's pricing page once "it depends on which model and how many credits" becomes the answer?

**Action you could take today:** If your product bundles AI into a flat subscription, pull usage data on your top 10% of AI users and model what a credit-based cost structure would do to their effective price. That's the group who will notice first.

### Karri Saarinen - Why AI-Assisted Product Development Risks Making Software Incoherent
**Source:** https://karrisaarinen.com/posts/someone-behind-the-software/
**Credibility:** High (design leader, co-founder of Linear, writing in his own voice about real product tradeoffs)

**What happened:** Karri Saarinen, co-founder and CEO of Linear, published a critical reflection on a trend he's seeing across products: features get added additively rather than intentionally, and he argues AI-assisted development accelerates this problem by making it cheap to ship "one more thing" without anyone forcing the hard tradeoff conversation. He frames it as a loss of "someone behind the software" — a point of view that says no as often as it says yes.

**Key patterns:**
- Additive feature creep: when building something is nearly free, the discipline of saying no to a feature disappears, and products accumulate capability without coherence
- AI lowers the cost of implementation but doesn't lower the cost of complexity — someone still has to hold the product's shape in their head
- Saarinen contrasts this with intentional design, where every addition is evaluated against what it costs the product's overall legibility, not just engineering time
- He ties this back to his own practice: a companion post from the same week describes how he now writes and builds his personal website entirely through Linear and AI agents, without local development setup, which makes the "who's the editor now" question concrete rather than abstract

**Why it matters for PMs:** This is the sharpest articulation yet of a problem every PM shipping with AI coding tools is going to hit: velocity stops being the constraint, and taste/judgment becomes the bottleneck instead. If your team can ship 10x more PRs because Cursor or Claude Code made implementation nearly free, the thing that used to gate scope (engineering capacity) is gone, and the thing that's left (do we actually want this) has to be enforced by someone, deliberately. Saarinen's point isn't "AI is bad for building," it's that AI removes a forcing function teams didn't realize they were relying on.

**Critical questions:**
- If engineering capacity is no longer the constraint, what replaces it as the thing that forces prioritization conversations on your team?
- Is "someone behind the software" a role (a single opinionated owner) or a process (a review gate), and which is more durable as teams scale AI-assisted development?
- How would you know if your own product has started accumulating additive, incoherent features, before users start telling you?

**Action you could take today:** Pull your last 90 days of shipped features and sort them into "subtracted something" vs. "only added something." If the ratio is heavily skewed toward addition, that's Saarinen's pattern showing up in your own roadmap.

## The Thread

**AI is quietly moving the real product work from "can we build it" to "should we, and who decides."** Notion's credit system is a pricing-layer admission that AI capability is now a managed resource, not a free perk. Karri Saarinen's post is the design-layer version of the same realization: when building is cheap, the scarce resource is judgment about what belongs in the product at all. Both are symptoms of the same shift this week's research keeps surfacing: the bottleneck in AI products isn't generation anymore, it's governance — of cost, of scope, of coherence.
