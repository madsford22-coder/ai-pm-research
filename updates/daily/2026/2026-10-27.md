# Daily PM Research Update: 2026-10-27

## The Short Version

Notion rebuilt its entire AI pricing model around a credit system while LangChain published the actual internal architecture of the agents running its marketing, sales, and engineering teams, and both are answering the same question from different directions: how do you make AI agents cheap enough and trustworthy enough to run constantly, not just on-demand.

### Notion - Rebuilt Notion AI Around Credits, Model Routing, and Long-Running Agents
**Source:** https://www.notion.com/blog/notion-ai-more-for-less
**Credibility:** High (first-party product announcement from co-founder Akshay Kotharis's team, paired with a technical deep-dive from the engineering team)

**What happened:** Notion rebuilt its AI product around three changes at once: a smarter model router that decides which model handles each request, long-running agents that can work in the background, and a bring-your-own-AI option that lets customers plug in their own model keys. The whole thing now runs on a credit system instead of flat per-seat AI pricing. A companion engineering post from Ghita Houir Alami explains how the router actually gets evaluated: pairwise comparisons and calibrated judging to measure model quality at scale, rather than trusting vendor benchmarks.

**Key technical details:**
- Model router picks between models per-request based on task complexity, not a single default model for all AI features
- Long-running agents can execute multi-step work without the user babysitting each step
- Bring-your-own-AI lets enterprise customers route requests through their own model provider contracts
- New credit-based pricing replaces the previous flat-rate AI add-on
- Router quality is measured via pairwise comparison testing and calibrated judge models, built in-house rather than relying on public benchmarks

**Why it matters for PMs:** This is the tell-tale sign a company has outgrown "just call one model for everything." If you're running an AI feature at any real scale, a credit system paired with a router is how you stop overpaying for the easy 80% of requests while still giving the hard 20% a capable model. The bring-your-own-AI option is also a trust signal: enterprise buyers increasingly want to control which model actually sees their data, not just trust that Notion picked a good one.

**Critical questions:**
- How does the credit system actually land for existing customers? Credits often mean price increases dressed up as flexibility.
- What's the actual cost delta between "smart routing" and "always use the best model"? Is this about user value or margin protection?
- Does bring-your-own-AI create a confusing support surface when something goes wrong and Notion didn't pick the model?

**Action you could take today:** If your product has a single default model for all AI requests, pull your request logs and bucket them by complexity. You're probably paying frontier-model prices for requests a cheaper model would handle fine.

### LangChain - Published the Five-Part Architecture Behind Its Internal Company Agents
**Source:** https://www.langchain.com/blog/components-building-company-harness
**Credibility:** High (first-party engineering post from Harrison Chase's team, describing production internal tooling)

**What happened:** LangChain documented the architecture pattern behind the agents it built for its own marketing, sales, and engineering teams, what Harrison Chase has been calling a "company harness" on X this week. The pattern has five parts: agents live inside Slack rather than a separate app, each agent handles identity per-tool rather than one shared credential, they keep both global and per-user memory, they execute code in a sandbox, and most of them run on a schedule rather than waiting to be invoked.

**Key technical details:**
- Agents are deployed inside Slack as the primary interface, not a standalone UI
- Per-tool identity handling means an agent's permissions are scoped to what each specific tool needs, not a single blanket credential
- Memory is split into global (shared context across the org) and per-user (personalized context) layers
- Code execution happens in a sandbox, isolating agent-run code from production systems
- Most agents are scheduled, running proactively rather than purely reactively
- A companion post, "How We Built Our Design Agent," shows a concrete instance: an open-source design agent in Slack that produces on-brand videos, graphics, and decks

**Why it matters for PMs:** This is a rare look at what "agents actually running in production inside a company" looks like, architecturally, from a team that builds agent infrastructure for a living. The scheduling detail matters most: most PM conversations about agents assume a user-initiated request-response loop, but LangChain's internal agents mostly just run, on a cadence, without anyone asking. That's a different design problem: observability, failure handling, and trust all look different when nobody's watching the agent work in real time.

**Critical questions:**
- What happens when a scheduled agent silently fails or does the wrong thing? Who notices, and how long does it take?
- Per-tool identity sounds secure in theory, how much engineering overhead does that scoping actually require to maintain as tools get added?
- Is "lives in Slack" a genuine product insight or just where LangChain's own team happens to work? Would this pattern hold for a company that runs on email or a ticketing system instead?

**Action you could take today:** Audit your own team's AI agent usage (if you have any) and ask which ones are reactive (wait to be asked) versus proactive (run on a schedule). If everything is reactive, you're missing the category of automation that actually saves time without someone remembering to ask for it.

### OpenAI - Published Usage Data From 165 Work Activities Across Nearly 100 Countries
**Source:** https://openai.com/index/chatgpt-expanding-world-of-work
**Credibility:** High (first-party data release, OpenAI Signals)

**What happened:** OpenAI released a data-driven look at how ChatGPT usage breaks down across work activities and geographies, tracking 165 distinct work activities in nearly 100 countries and how usage patterns shift over time. This is the kind of longitudinal usage data that's rare to get from a foundation model company directly, most of what we see is anecdote or isolated case study.

**Key technical details:**
- Data spans 165 distinct categorized "work activities," not just broad categories like "coding" or "writing"
- Coverage across nearly 100 countries, allowing geographic comparison of adoption patterns
- Framed as showing change over time, not just a snapshot, implying OpenAI is tracking activity-level usage shifts longitudinally

**Why it matters for PMs:** If you're building any product that touches "AI for work," this is closer to primary market research than you'll usually get for free. Knowing which work activities are growing or plateauing in ChatGPT usage, and where geographically, is a direct input into where unmet demand might exist for a more specialized tool. The risk is taking OpenAI's framing at face value: this is also OpenAI making the case that ChatGPT is embedded everywhere, which serves their enterprise sales narrative.

**Critical questions:**
- Is "165 work activities" OpenAI's own taxonomy? How would a different categorization change the story?
- Does rising usage in a category mean the market is being served, or that there's a gap a focused competitor could exploit?
- How much of this is organic usage versus enterprise deployments OpenAI is counting as "work"?

**Action you could take today:** If you have access to OpenAI's full Signals report, cross-reference the top-growing work activities in your market against your own product roadmap. A category growing fast in ChatGPT usage but still clunky there is a real opportunity signal.

## The Thread

**Agents are moving from "answer when asked" to "run on a schedule and just work."** LangChain's internal agents mostly run proactively, Notion built long-running agents into its core product, and OpenAI is pushing GPT-6.1 Terra's computer-use into default availability across every major platform within a day of launch. The design problem PMs now need to solve isn't "how do we respond to a user's agent request," it's "how do we build trust and observability into something that acts without being asked."
