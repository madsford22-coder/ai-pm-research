# Daily PM Research Update: 2026-09-09

## The Short Version

Two things happened today that point at the same tension: Julie Zhuo wrote about wanting to ditch third-party software entirely because vibe coding makes custom tools viable, and Pieter Levels published the receipts — $25K/month in SaaS replaced by tools he built himself. Meanwhile, LangChain shipped a meaningful auth primitive for multi-agent systems, and GPT-6 Astra landing on Amazon Bedrock closes a gap that was annoying enterprise teams building on AWS.

### Julie Zhuo — "I Never Want to Use Third-Party Software Again"
**Source:** https://joulee.medium.com/i-never-want-to-use-third-party-software-again-6309d6aea5de
**Credibility:** High (first-party post from a well-regarded former VP of Design at Meta, now a PM and product thinker)

**What happened:** Julie Zhuo published a piece articulating something that's been building quietly in the practitioner community: vibe coding has crossed a threshold where the friction of adopting, learning, integrating, and paying for third-party software is now often higher than just building what you need. She's not arguing for reinventing everything — she's arguing that the calculus has shifted, and for many internal tools and workflows, custom-built is now the rational choice.

**Key patterns:**
- The value proposition of SaaS has always been "faster than building, cheaper than hiring engineers." Both of those assumptions are weakening as AI lowers the cost of custom development significantly.
- Third-party software comes with opinionated workflows, vendor lock-in, and integration overhead. Custom tools can be shaped exactly to how a team actually works.
- This isn't about consumer products or mission-critical systems — it's about the long tail of internal tools, dashboards, and workflow software that teams have historically tolerated off-the-shelf solutions for.
- The shift mirrors what happened with open-source: once the build cost dropped, "good enough" SaaS stopped being good enough.

**Why it matters for PMs:** This is a product category threat that most SaaS PMs are not taking seriously enough yet. If your product is primarily used for internal workflows — project tracking, reporting, light data transformation, team communication tooling — you are now competing with "I'll just build this myself in a weekend." Zhuo putting this sentiment into words gives it legitimacy and reach. The PMs who should be most worried are those building horizontal productivity tools where switching costs are low and the workflow isn't particularly complex. The ones who should take note as an opportunity: tools that genuinely require scale, reliability, or network effects that a vibe-coded alternative can't replicate.

**Critical questions:**
- Where is the line between "vibe-codeable in a weekend" and "actually requires a mature product"? Zhuo's argument is compelling but risks overgeneralizing — teams have tried to build internal tools forever and usually end up with unmaintained junk.
- Does this apply to B2C products, or is it really only a threat to B2B internal-use software?
- What's the maintenance burden of vibe-coded internal tools over 18 months? The build cost is down; the ownership cost might not be.
- How should SaaS PMs respond? Is there a version of "easier to configure than to build" that re-establishes the value prop?

**Action you could take today:** Audit one tool your team uses primarily for internal workflows and estimate: (a) how many hours it would take to vibe-code a 70%-good replacement, and (b) what your team actually pays for it annually. The gap between those numbers is your exposure.

### Pieter Levels — $25K/Month in SaaS Replaced by Vibe-Coded Services
**Source:** https://levels.io/replaced-saas-with-vibe-coded-services
**Credibility:** High (first-party account from a builder who does this publicly and with receipts)

**What happened:** Pieter Levels published the full breakdown of SaaS tools he's replaced with services he built himself using AI-assisted development. The headline number is $25,000/month in savings. One specific example he also published today: he replaced a $249/month Scrapingbee plan with a custom scraper that costs him $1/month. This isn't hypothetical — he runs multiple live products and is sharing actual costs and implementations.

**Key patterns:**
- The Scrapingbee replacement is a clean illustration of the dynamic: a niche B2B SaaS that solved a real but narrow problem is now directly replicable by its customers at 1/250th the cost.
- The $25K/month figure is attention-grabbing but the more interesting signal is the *type* of tools being replaced: they're almost all in the "solves one specific problem, charges a monthly fee, moderate switching cost" category.
- Levels is not a typical enterprise buyer — he's a solo founder who can absorb the maintenance overhead personally. But this behavior is spreading to small teams.

**Why it matters for PMs:** This is Zhuo's thesis with a dollar amount attached. The categories most at risk are single-function API services, lightly-differentiated SaaS tools, and anything where the core value is "we handle the complexity so you don't have to" — because AI is now handling that complexity directly. If you're a PM at a tool in this category, the question isn't whether this is happening; it's how fast and to which segments first.

**Critical questions:**
- At what team size does vibe-coded infrastructure become unsustainable? Levels can do this solo; a 20-person startup probably can't.
- Are there categories of SaaS that are *immune* to this — and what makes them immune? (Network effects, regulatory requirements, data moats?)
- What's the right strategic response for a SaaS PM whose product is in the crosshairs? Double down on integration, reliability, and support? Or pivot to more complex, defensible functionality?

**Action you could take today:** If you work in B2B SaaS, go look at your churn data and see if there's a signal of customers canceling and citing "built our own." If you're a PM at a product company using SaaS tools, try the Levels exercise: pick your most expensive single-purpose tool and give an engineer two hours with Claude to see how far they get on a replacement.

### LangChain — Connections: Managed Credentials and Per-Caller Identity for Managed Deep Agents
**Source:** https://www.langchain.com/blog/connections-managed-credentials-and-per-caller-identity-for-managed-deep-agents
**Credibility:** High (first-party product announcement from LangChain)

**What happened:** LangChain shipped "Connections" — a credential management primitive for their Managed Deep Agents platform. The key capability: agents can now act with each caller's identity through per-user OAuth. Instead of every agent action happening under a single service account, the agent can authenticate as the specific user who invoked it, respecting that user's permissions across connected systems.

**Key technical details:**
- Per-user OAuth means an agent can call downstream services (email, calendar, CRM, etc.) with the invoking user's credentials, not a shared service account.
- This solves a real production problem: shared service accounts give agents too much access and make audit trails useless.
- Credential management is handled at the platform level, so developers don't have to build token storage, refresh logic, or scope management themselves.
- This is part of the Managed Deep Agents product, which is LangChain's hosted/managed layer on top of their agent infrastructure.

**Why it matters for PMs:** The hardest part of shipping agentic features at enterprise scale isn't the AI — it's the auth. When an agent takes an action, who did it? Whose permissions apply? What's in the audit log? These are the questions that slow enterprise AI deals to a crawl. LangChain just made one of those questions substantially easier to answer. If you're building agents that touch any third-party system with user-level permissions (and almost all useful enterprise agents do), this is infrastructure you'd otherwise have to build yourself. The build-vs-buy calculus on LangSmith/LangChain just shifted for teams that were holding out because they didn't want to hand credential management to a third party — now the credential management IS the reason to use the platform.

**Critical questions:**
- How does this interact with enterprise SSO requirements? If a company mandates Okta for everything, does per-user OAuth still work cleanly?
- What's the scope management story — can admins restrict which OAuth scopes agents can request on behalf of users?
- Does this only work with the Managed Deep Agents product, or can it be used with self-hosted LangChain deployments?
- What happens when a user revokes their OAuth token mid-workflow? Does the agent fail gracefully or leave things in a broken state?

**Action you could take today:** If you're building any agentic workflow that calls downstream APIs on behalf of users, write down exactly how you're handling auth right now. If the answer is "shared service account," that's a security and compliance gap worth fixing — and LangChain Connections is now a concrete option to evaluate.

## The Thread

**The build-vs-buy calculus is actively repricing.** This week's signal isn't just Zhuo's essay or Levels' receipts in isolation — it's that both are describing the same shift from different vantage points (one conceptual, one with actual dollar figures), and LangChain's Connections feature lands in the same week as the clearest evidence yet that the infrastructure layer is maturing fast enough to support the shift. The open question this raises: which category of SaaS is *defensible* against this, and what makes it so? Network effects, regulatory moats, and data accumulation are the usual answers — but "the product is too complex to replicate in a weekend" is starting to sound like something companies say right before someone replicates it in a weekend.

## Sit With This

Julie Zhuo argues that vibe coding has changed the build-vs-buy calculus for internal tools — and Pieter Levels published $25K/month in receipts to back it up. The threat is real for single-function SaaS, but the defense usually cited is "complexity, reliability, and maintenance over time."

**For your product:** Pick the one feature or workflow your users rely on most. Could a motivated engineer vibe-code a 70%-good replacement in a week? If yes, what's actually keeping your users from doing that — and is that reason durable?
