# Daily PM Research Update: 2026-11-12

## The Short Version

AWS spent the day publishing an entire playbook for making agent costs legible (per-task ROI, per-IAM-principal spend caps, four inference levers that quietly inflate your Bedrock bill), while LangChain made the case that eval-driven development is the only way to actually know if your agent got better, and Notion's engineering team showed what it looks like to build that measurement muscle in-house with a model router that grades its own outputs.

## Items

### AWS - Four Bedrock inference choices that quietly change your cost per task
**Source:** https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/four-amazon-bedrock-inference-choices-that-affect-cost/
**Credibility:** High (first-party technical blog with methodology)

**What happened:** AWS published a detailed breakdown of four request-level settings in Bedrock, cache duration, service tier, reasoning effort, and maxTokens, that each independently change how many tokens get processed and what rate you're charged, even after you've already tuned your prompts. This dropped alongside a second post on tracking and capping Bedrock spend per IAM principal using CUR 2.0 and AWS Budgets, and a third on calculating true cost-per-task across "four hidden cost drivers." Three posts, one day, one theme: agent cost is not a line item, it's a system with multiple knobs that fight each other.

**Key technical details:**
- Cache duration affects how much of your context gets re-processed versus pulled from cache at a cheaper rate
- Service tier (e.g., standard vs. priority) changes the per-token rate independent of the model you chose
- Reasoning effort settings can silently multiply token consumption for the same nominal request
- maxTokens caps don't just limit output, they interact with the other three levers to change total cost
- The companion post shows how to attribute spend to specific IAM principals via tags and enforce hard caps with Budgets actions that trigger a Deny policy

**Why it matters for PMs:** If you're shipping an agent feature and your cost model is "price per token times expected volume," you're going to be wrong, possibly by a lot. These four levers mean two teams running the "same" model can have wildly different unit economics based on settings most PMs never see in a roadmap doc. This is also a build-vs-buy signal: AWS is explicitly building the FinOps tooling layer for agents because customers kept getting surprised by their bills, which tells you this problem is common enough to productize.

**Critical questions:**
- Who on your team actually owns these four settings, engineering or whoever owns the cost center? If nobody owns them, nobody's optimizing them.
- Does your pricing model for an AI feature account for the fact that reasoning effort can vary per-request, not just per-model?
- If you switched service tiers tomorrow to cut costs, would you know the latency or quality tradeoff before your users did?

**Action you could take today:** Pull your last month of Bedrock (or equivalent provider) billing and check whether you can even see a breakdown by these four levers. If you can't, that's your first finding.

### LangChain - Eval-driven development as a named practice
**Source:** https://www.langchain.com/blog/what-is-eval-driven-development
**Credibility:** High (first-party, but prescriptive rather than data-backed)

**What happened:** LangChain formalized "eval-driven development" as a practice: define what good agent behavior looks like, write the eval before you write the change, then compare experiments against that baseline instead of eyeballing outputs. It's the agent-era analog of test-driven development, and it's notable that LangChain is naming and packaging it as a discipline rather than a nice-to-have.

**Key technical details:**
- The workflow order is: define success criteria → write the eval → make the change → run the comparison
- Positioned explicitly as a replacement for ad hoc "looks better to me" evaluation of agent outputs
- Published the same week as Shreya Shankar's "Evals for AI Engineers" book launch event (Nov 12, Mountain View, with Hamel Husain), which is the more rigorous, practitioner-driven version of the same argument

**Why it matters for PMs:** Every agent feature ships with an invisible quality regression risk: a prompt tweak, a model swap, a new tool definition can silently make things worse for a segment of users while looking fine in your spot-checks. If your team doesn't have a named, repeatable eval process, "did this get better" is answered by vibes. This is the same conversation as the AWS cost posts, just for quality instead of dollars: agent systems need their own measurement infrastructure, not hand-me-downs from traditional software QA.

**Critical questions:**
- Do you have a written definition of "good" for your agent's core task, or does that live in one engineer's head?
- When was the last time a prompt or model change shipped without a before/after eval comparison?
- Is your eval set actually representative of your hardest real user queries, or is it the easy cases you happened to write down first?

**Action you could take today:** Pick one agent feature in production and write down, in one sentence each, three things that would make an output "bad." That's the start of an eval set.

### Notion - Inside the model router that grades its own answers
**Source:** https://www.notion.com/blog/inside-notions-upgraded-model-router-auto
**Credibility:** High (first-party engineering post, named author)

**What happened:** Ghita Houir Alami, an engineering manager at Notion, published a technical deep dive on how Notion's "Auto" model router now uses pairwise comparisons and calibrated judging to measure model quality at scale, rather than relying on static benchmarks. This is the practical, shipped version of the "eval-driven development" argument LangChain is making in the abstract: Notion built the measurement system first, then used it to decide which model answers which request.

**Key technical details:**
- Router uses pairwise comparison (model A's answer vs. model B's answer) rather than absolute scoring
- Judging is "calibrated," meaning the system accounts for known biases in how a judge model rates outputs
- This upgraded router is the backbone of Notion's recently announced credit-based pricing system, where routing decisions directly determine cost-per-query
- Published alongside Notion's broader "next era" repositioning as "the best context system for every agent on the planet"

**Why it matters for PMs:** This is worth flagging because it's a woman-led engineering team solving the exact cost-and-quality tension the AWS and LangChain posts describe today, but doing it with production data instead of a framework post. The model router is also a direct monetization lever: if the router is good, Notion can route cheap queries to cheap models and expensive queries to expensive models, which is exactly how they justify a credit system instead of flat per-seat AI pricing. That's a pattern worth studying if you're building any multi-model product.

**Critical questions:**
- How do you validate that a calibrated judge model isn't just inheriting the same blind spots as the models it's grading?
- If your product routes between models for cost reasons, do users ever notice quality drift, and how would you know?
- Is pairwise comparison practical at your query volume, or does it only work because Notion has enough traffic to make it statistically meaningful?

**Action you could take today:** If you route between multiple models anywhere in your product, ask your team how routing decisions are currently validated, benchmark scores, vibes, or actual comparative evals, and write down the honest answer.

## The Thread

**Cost and quality measurement for agents is becoming its own discipline, not an afterthought.** This week alone: AWS published three separate posts on making Bedrock spend legible and attributable, LangChain named "eval-driven development" as a formal practice, Shreya Shankar launched a book specifically on evals for AI engineers, and Notion detailed the calibrated-judging system behind their model router. None of these are launches, they're all teams admitting that "ship the agent" was the easy part, and "know if it's working and what it costs" is the actual hard problem nobody solved yet.

## Sit With This

Notion's model router uses pairwise comparison and calibrated judging to decide which model handles which query, and that routing decision now directly determines what a credit costs the user under their new pricing system.

**For your product:** If you route between models (or providers, or reasoning effort levels) anywhere in your stack, could you currently prove to a skeptical exec that the routing logic optimizes for user-perceived quality and not just your margin?
