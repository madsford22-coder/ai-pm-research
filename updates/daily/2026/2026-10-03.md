# Daily PM Research Update: 2026-10-03

## The Short Version

Notion shipped the infrastructure layer for agentic work (shared skills libraries, persistent agent memory) in the same week Teresa Torres published a deep case study on Vistaly rebuilding its core workflow around AI-assisted interview synthesis, and LangChain quietly dropped a 64% cost reduction on coding tasks just by routing models smarter, three signals pointing at the same thing: the agent tooling conversation is moving from "can it do the task" to "how do we govern, remember, and afford doing the task at scale."

### Notion - A Skills Library for Every Agent
**Source:** https://www.notion.com/blog/a-skills-library-for-every-agent
**Credibility:** High (first-party product announcement)

**What happened:** Notion launched a skills library that lets teams manage agent skills using Notion's existing editing, collaboration, and permissions model, then deploy those skills across any agent that connects to Notion. Geoffrey Litt (tracked person, now at Notion) is credited on the post. It ships alongside a companion feature, "Lore," which turns agent conversations, decisions, and tasks into durable Notion pages that future agents and humans can both reference.

**Key technical details:**
- Skills are authored and versioned like any other Notion page, with the same commenting, permissions, and history
- Skills library is designed to be used "everywhere" an agent connects, not just inside Notion's own AI features
- Lore (the shared memory layer) persists conversations, decisions, and "durable facts" as structured Notion pages rather than ephemeral chat logs
- Both features build on Notion's existing Custom Agents and MCP connections, which the company separately detailed in a case study about how its own Customer Education team uses agents to keep Academy content current

**Why it matters for PMs:** This is the team-infrastructure problem for agents, the same one Wispr Flow solved for voice tools a few weeks back. An individual can prompt an agent effectively, but a team can't scale that without a shared place to store "what good looks like." Notion turning its own document model into the skills and memory substrate for agents is a strong bet that governance and knowledge-sharing, not raw capability, is the next bottleneck for agentic adoption inside companies.

**Critical questions:**
- Does a skills library actually get maintained, or does it rot the way wikis and runbooks always have?
- How does Lore handle conflicting or outdated "durable facts" as decisions change over time?
- Is this a moat for Notion, or does every agent platform need to build an equivalent and this just becomes commodity infrastructure?

**Action you could take today:** If your team is already using Custom Agents or MCP connections into Notion, audit what "tribal knowledge" currently lives only in Slack threads or people's heads, and see if it's a candidate for a first skill or Lore entry.

### Teresa Torres - How Vistaly Rebuilt Its Product Around AI Interview Synthesis
**Source:** https://www.producttalk.org/generating-opportunity-solution-trees-with-ai-how-vistaly-rebuilt-its-product-around-interview-synthesis-evals-and-repair-loops/
**Credibility:** High (detailed case study from a recognized PM discovery expert)

**What happened:** Teresa Torres published a case study on Vistaly, which rebuilt its product around AI-generated opportunity solution trees, built directly from interview synthesis. The piece covers how Vistaly approached evals and "repair loops" to keep AI-generated trees trustworthy rather than just plausible-sounding.

**Key [discovery] patterns:**
- Interview synthesis feeding directly into opportunity solution tree generation, rather than requiring a human to manually extract themes first
- Evals built specifically to check whether AI-generated trees reflect what was actually said in interviews, not just coherent-sounding opportunities
- "Repair loops" as a named mechanism for catching and correcting AI misreads of interview data before they propagate into product decisions

**Why it matters for PMs:** Discovery work is exactly the kind of judgment-heavy process people assumed AI couldn't touch. Torres and Vistaly show a credible middle path: use AI to compress the mechanical synthesis work, but build explicit evaluation and correction loops so a hallucinated opportunity doesn't quietly become a roadmap bet. This is a concrete answer to "how do PMs keep judgment in the loop" that doesn't rely on hand-waving.

**Critical questions:**
- What's the failure rate of AI-generated opportunity trees before repair loops catch the error, and who's checking?
- Does this scale to messy, contradictory interview data, or does it work best on clean, well-structured sessions?
- How much of the "rebuilt the product around" framing is Vistaly's own tool versus a generalizable practice any PM team could adopt with existing tools?

**Action you could take today:** If you're running customer interviews, try feeding transcripts into an LLM to draft a first-pass opportunity list, then explicitly compare it against the raw transcript yourself as a one-person "eval" before trusting it.

### LangChain - 64% Cost Reduction From a Model Router, No Quality Drop
**Source:** https://www.langchain.com/blog/how-to-build-a-model-router-in-the-harness
**Credibility:** High (first-party engineering writeup with a specific, falsifiable metric)

**What happened:** LangChain built a model router into Open SWE's coding harness that picks which model handles a given task, and reports it cut median cost per coding task by 64% with no measurable drop in quality.

**Key technical details:**
- The router sits in the "harness," the layer that wraps a coding agent and decides how to execute a task, not inside the model itself
- Routing decisions are made per-task rather than per-session, implying some tasks route to cheaper models and others to more expensive ones based on complexity signals
- LangChain frames this as a reusable pattern ("how to build your own"), not a one-off optimization specific to Open SWE

**Why it matters for PMs:** A 64% cost cut with no quality loss is the kind of number that should change how you think about "just use the best model for everything." If routing is this cheap to build and this effective, it becomes a default architectural choice for any AI product with meaningfully varied task complexity, not just a nice-to-have for cost-sensitive teams. It also reframes the build-vs-buy question: the router, not the model, may be where the real product differentiation lives.

**Critical questions:**
- What's the actual task mix being routed, and does "no measurable drop in quality" hold on the hardest 10% of tasks, or just the easy majority?
- How much engineering investment did the router itself require, and does that investment pay off at smaller scale than Open SWE's?
- Does routing complexity introduce new failure modes (wrong model picked for a task) that aren't captured by aggregate quality metrics?

**Action you could take today:** Pull your product's AI feature logs and segment tasks by complexity or cost; even a rough split (simple vs. complex) could reveal whether you're overpaying by sending every request to your most expensive model.

## The Thread

**The infrastructure layer for agents is the story this week, not the agents themselves.** Notion's skills library and shared memory, LangChain's model router cutting costs 64%, and Linear's bug autofix loop all point the same direction: the interesting product work has shifted from "can an agent do this task" to "how do we govern what it knows, route what it costs, and remember what it decided." Torres's Vistaly case study is the discovery-side mirror of this, evals and repair loops instead of routers and skills libraries, but the same underlying shift: trust and scale, not raw capability, are what teams are building for now.
