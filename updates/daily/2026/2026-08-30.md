# Daily PM Research Update: 2026-08-30

## The Short Version

Tara Seshan (OpenAI's product lead for Codex and ChatGPT Work) lays out a "three eras of AI" framing that's the clearest articulation I've seen of where product strategy needs to go next. Meanwhile, Pieter Levels shipped a live AI-generated infinite stream called Infinite Slop, 37,000 people watched it, and it accidentally became a signal about video AI product velocity. Anthropic opened a research preview of something called the Model Hardware Standard — a spec for AI agents operating physical hardware — and it's the most quietly significant thing I saw this week.

### Lenny's Newsletter / Tara Seshan (OpenAI) - AI's Third Era: The Rise of Persistent AI Coworkers

**Source:** https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/ais-third-era-the-rise-of-persistent
**Credibility:** High (Tara Seshan is OpenAI's head of Codex and ChatGPT Work, published on Lenny's Newsletter, a primary source for PM craft thinking)

**What happened:** Tara Seshan, who leads Codex and ChatGPT Work at OpenAI, went on Lenny's podcast to lay out a framework for where AI product development is heading. The core framing: we've moved through two eras (AI as tool, AI as assistant) and are entering a third — AI as persistent coworker. The key shift isn't capability, it's continuity. The excerpt calls out "steering vs. rowing" as the central metaphor: PMs and product teams need to stop rowing (doing the work) and start steering (directing AI that does it).

She also makes a pointed claim: **ambition is now the differentiator.** Not intelligence, not tooling. The bottleneck is whether you're willing to let AI take on more than you're comfortable with. And she argues you need to build for where models will be in two to three years, not where they are today.

**Key patterns:**
- Three era framework: Tool (autocomplete, search) → Assistant (chat, Q&A) → Persistent Coworker (async, agentic, context-aware over time)
- "Steering vs. rowing" as the PM's new job description
- Ambition as the differentiator: the bottleneck is organizational and psychological, not technical
- Build for model trajectory, not current state — decisions you make today will be executed by models that are dramatically more capable

**Why it matters for PMs:** This framing is worth internalizing before your next roadmap cycle. If you're still designing AI features as single-session tools, you're building for the second era. Persistent coworkers require different architecture decisions: memory, state, identity, trust levels, error recovery. The "steering vs. rowing" shift also has implications for how you write specs and define done — if the AI is doing the work, your job is to define the outcome and course-correct, not to detail every step.

The "build for where models will be" argument is the hardest one to sell internally, but Seshan's framing gives you a clean way to defend longer bets: you're not over-engineering for today, you're de-risking for the model trajectory that's already underway.

**Critical questions:**
- How do you design trust and error recovery for persistent AI coworkers when users aren't present for each action? What's the right intervention surface?
- "Ambition as differentiator" is a compelling narrative, but what does it look like concretely — which product decisions require more ambition, and where does ambition become recklessness?
- Building for model trajectory assumes you can predict it. What happens when a two-to-three year bet is wrong because models plateau in a specific domain?
- If persistent coworkers require memory and state, what are the privacy and data governance implications for enterprise products?

**Action you could take today:** Pull up your current AI feature roadmap and identify which items are "era two" (single-session, assistant-mode) features. Flag the ones that assume a session boundary exists — those are the first candidates to rethink if persistent coworker architecture becomes the standard.

### Pieter Levels - Infinite Slop: A Live AI-Generated Stream That 37,000 People Watched

**Source:** https://levels.io/i-built-infinite-slop (build post) and https://levels.io/37000-watched-infinite-slop (follow-up)
**Credibility:** Medium-High (first-party, Pieter Levels is a well-documented indie builder with a track record of shipping and publishing real metrics)

**What happened:** Pieter Levels built and shipped Infinite Slop: an infinite, interactive, AI-generated live stream. Users watch and can interact with an endlessly generated video environment. It went live, 37,000 people tuned in, and Levels followed up by registering InfiniteSlop.ai. The build post documents how it was made; the follow-up is essentially a signal that demand validated the concept fast enough to warrant turning it into a real product.

The timing matters. Earlier this week, Levels also posted that AI video generation is now faster than real-time playback. Infinite Slop is the product expression of that observation — if you can generate video faster than people watch it, what do you build? His answer: an infinite stream.

**Key patterns:**
- Real-time AI video generation is now fast enough for live streaming use cases — this is a capability inflection, not just a quality improvement
- The "infinite" format solves a content cost problem: no writers, no production, no library. Generation is the library.
- 37,000 viewers is a real adoption signal for a solo-built product with no marketing budget — this is not a niche
- Levels registered the domain after seeing the numbers, classic lean validation loop in public

**Why it matters for PMs:** Two things here. First, the capability signal: if video generation is now faster than real-time, the constraint that limited live AI video has flipped. Features that required pre-generation are now live-generation candidates. That's worth pressure-testing in your roadmap if you're anywhere near video or streaming. Second, the product signal: infinite generative content is a new content format, not just a demo. Someone is going to build a real business on this. The question is whether it's a feature for an existing video platform (YouTube, Twitch) or a standalone product. Levels is treating it as the latter.

**Critical questions:**
- 37,000 viewers is impressive for a solo launch, but what's the retention pattern? Did people watch for 30 seconds or 30 minutes? The business model is completely different depending on the answer.
- If infinite AI video becomes a content format, what are the moderation implications? Generative content at streaming scale with real-time interaction is a trust and safety problem that hasn't been solved yet.
- Does "Infinite Slop" as a name intentionally signal low quality as part of the joke, or does quality eventually need to improve for retention? What's the quality bar for this format?
- How does this interact with existing content platforms' incentive structures? Would YouTube or Twitch want to host AI-generated infinite streams, or does this require a standalone platform?

**Action you could take today:** If you work on any product adjacent to video, streaming, or content generation, pull the current state of video generation latency for the providers you use or are evaluating. Check whether real-time generation is now viable for your use case — the constraint may have changed without your roadmap reflecting it.

### Anthropic - Model Hardware Standard Research Preview

**Source:** https://www.anthropic.com/news (referenced in company data as "Previewing the Model Hardware Standard," published Aug 27, 2026)
**Credibility:** High (first-party Anthropic announcement, research preview)

**What happened:** Anthropic opened a research preview of the Model Hardware Standard (MHS), described as "a shared specification for AI agents to safely operate physical hardware." This is not a consumer product launch — it's a spec document being opened to research review. But what it signals is significant: Anthropic is working on standardizing how AI agents interact with physical hardware, not just software APIs.

The framing is around safety. The MHS is positioned as infrastructure for AI agents operating in physical environments — think robotics, industrial control, manufacturing — where mistakes have physical consequences. Opening it as a research preview suggests they're looking for feedback from the research and enterprise communities before committing to a standard.

**Key patterns:**
- Anthropic is moving from language model provider to standards body for physical AI agent infrastructure
- The "safety-first" framing is consistent with their brand, but also strategic: if Anthropic sets the safety spec, they influence what "safe physical AI" means
- A shared specification implies they want other companies to adopt it — this is ecosystem play, not just a product feature
- Research preview stage means this is at least 12-18 months from being production-relevant for most products

**Why it matters for PMs:** This is a long horizon signal, but it's the kind of signal worth putting on your radar now. If you're building in any industry that involves physical operations — logistics, manufacturing, healthcare equipment, field services — the question of "how do AI agents safely interact with physical systems" is coming your way. Anthropic's attempt to standardize that interface could make the build-vs-buy decision easier (adopt the standard) or harder (wait and see if it wins).

More immediately, the pattern is instructive: Anthropic is using research previews to shape standards before they ship products. That's a specific type of competitive move worth understanding. The company that defines the safety spec often defines what "compliant" means — and that's a durable moat.

**Critical questions:**
- Who are the target adopters of the MHS? Robotics companies? Industrial automation? Healthcare device makers? The standard's value depends entirely on whether those buyers actually adopt it.
- Is this a genuine safety effort or a standards-setting competitive move? The two aren't mutually exclusive, but the answer affects how other companies should respond.
- What's the relationship between MHS and existing industrial safety standards (IEC 62443, ISO 13849)? Does this complement them or try to replace them?
- What enforcement or certification mechanism, if any, will the MHS include? A spec without adoption pressure is just a document.

**Action you could take today:** If your product roadmap includes any agentic features that interact with physical systems or operational workflows (not just software), flag the MHS research preview as a dependency to watch. Add it to your competitive landscape notes and revisit in Q1 2027 when it may have more adoption signal.

## Sit With This

Tara Seshan's "steering vs. rowing" framing is clean, but it implies a real transfer of execution to AI — which means a real change in how you verify work is done correctly.

**For your product:** Pick one AI-assisted workflow your team or your users currently run through a single session (a task started and finished in one chat, one prompt, one tool use). If that workflow became persistent — the AI picks it up, runs it in the background, delivers output later — what would have to be true about your error handling, your trust model, and your user notification design for that to feel safe rather than scary?
