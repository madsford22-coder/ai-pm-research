# August 2026 Research Summary

August was the month AI tooling stopped being individual utilities and became team infrastructure. Perplexity turned Computer into a multiplayer agentic OS with persistent memory and shared projects. AWS shipped deterministic policy controls (Dogwood) for agents running in production. Vercel made approval gates a first-class primitive in agent workflows. The throughline is consistent: companies figured out that individual AI tools hit a ceiling, and the stickiness lives at the team layer where context, memory, and coordination actually matter.

There's a second tension running underneath this month's announcements: who controls the resources and at what level of the stack? Atlassian capped per-employee AI token budgets. LangSmith shipped spend governance for coding agents. Vercel's AI Gateway added cost observability logs. The "free to experiment" phase of enterprise AI is closing, and the "who owns what, and what does it cost" phase is opening.

## What Matters

- **Individual AI tools are becoming team infrastructure through shared state and persistent memory.** Perplexity's Projects adds persistent memory and files scoped to a project that multiple users access — turning Computer from a personal browser into a shared workspace. This is the exact pattern Wispr Flow ran with Team Dictionary and Notion ran when it moved from personal workspace to team wiki. The moment context and memory persist across users instead of resetting per session, the tool stops being optional for individuals and starts being required for the team. Pricing and permissioning will determine whether this holds at enterprise scale, but the product move is clear: the retention and revenue live at the team layer, not the personal layer.

- **Enterprise AI deployments are being unblocked by policy-as-code and cost controls at the infrastructure layer.** AWS shipped Dogwood — an open source policy language for defining what AI agents can and cannot do across multi-step action sequences. This lets you enforce rules like "don't approve a financial transfer unless a human review happened in this session" or "cap total spending across this agent's entire run." LangChain shipped runtime spend controls for production agents. Vercel added cost observability logs to AI Gateway. These aren't features for end users — they're the answer to the three questions that actually kill enterprise AI deals: "Can we cap spending?" "Can we ensure compliance?" "Can we audit what happened?" The fact that Dogwood is open source matters: if it becomes the standard way to express agent constraints (like OPA did for infrastructure policy), it's not lock-in — it's an industry pattern.

- **The cost of building is reshaping what PMs actually do.** Stripe built Kai, a company-wide AI agent reaching 5,000 users, in roughly one week on LangChain's Deep Agents. Lenny Rachitsky built a working PR review bot in 30 minutes using Vercel Eve and Codex. Ravi Mehta published that the traditional PM artifact cycle (brief, PRD, design review, spec, build) was a hedge against expensive development — when dev costs drop, the whole pipeline changes. Tom Verrilli (CPO of Whatnot) went on Lenny's podcast and said he regrets that product management exists as a distinct function, arguing that fewer, more senior PMs doing real IC work outperform any team structured around headcount ratios. When development is cheap and fast, being a blocker to building is a real liability. The PM role isn't disappearing, but its shape is changing: from coordinator to decision-maker, from artifact-producer to taste and judgment.
