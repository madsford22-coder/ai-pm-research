# September 2026 Research Summary

The month's clearest signal is uncomfortable: AI agents are capable enough to get real work done in production, but we haven't built the product, governance, or trust infrastructure to make that safe or repeatable at scale. OpenAI's agents coordinated through public wikis, Perplexity handed GPT-6 Astra partial control of production systems, and Anthropic's CEO publicly called for slowing down — all in the same month. The tension between capability and safety is no longer theoretical.

The practical implication cuts through all the noise: the teams winning with AI right now aren't the ones with the best models. They're the ones who've figured out how to make capability visible, repeatable, and trustworthy. That's a product and design problem, not a model problem — and it's where the real differentiation is happening.

## What Matters

- **Agent interfaces are too thin to support real collaboration.** Maggie Appleton's essay on "boundary objects" exposes a core UX failure: most agent products show a chat window and a final output, but nothing of the reasoning, constraints, or tradeoffs in between. Teams building planning agents (trip planning, project management, research synthesis) are shipping interfaces that prevent genuine human-agent collaboration. The products that win here won't be the ones with the most capable reasoning — they'll be the ones that surface intermediate state in ways humans can actually engage with.

- **"Sometimes works" is not the same as "reliably works," and your benchmarks won't tell you the difference.** IBM Research's ALTK-Evolve framework reveals the consistency problem: an agent that passes 80% of tasks might be hitting different tasks right on different runs rather than being reliably stable on any single task. Thomas Wolf surfaced this as the actual blocker for production agent deployment. This is why Maggie Appleton's interface argument matters — if users can't see what the agent is actually doing, they can't calibrate their trust to the agent's real reliability.

- **The build-vs-buy calculus for internal tools and infrastructure just inverted.** Julie Zhuo, Pieter Levels, and Stripe's engineering team all published signals about the same shift in September: vibe-coding has made custom-built tools cheaper than SaaS subscriptions for specific categories (scraping, monitoring, data transformation), AI adoption without behavioral change produces compliance theater not value, and enterprise-scale AI requires building a three-layer infrastructure (context, governance, shared skills) rather than just handing people tools. Levels replaced $25K/month in SaaS with vibe-coded alternatives; the infrastructure teams winning are the ones building governance at the point of use (Jamf's per-user spend enforcement, Cursor's self-hosted machines, Perplexity's hybrid local/cloud compute).

## Essential Resources (3)

1. **Maggie Appleton** — [Planning with Agents: Divided Worlds, Boundary Objects, and Thicker Interfaces](https://maggieappleton.com/planning-agents/) — The core UX insight for any product with agents doing planning: the interface needs a shared artifact (a "boundary object") that humans and agents can both see and modify, not just a chat history.

2. **Teresa Torres** — [AI Evals: A Hands-On Guide for Product Teams](https://www.producttalk.org/ai-evals/) — The practical framework PMs actually need to measure whether AI features are getting better: error analysis first, then structured evals, with a downloadable test harness included.

3. **AWS / Jamf** — [Tokenomics at Scale: How Jamf Built Real-Time Spend Enforcement for Amazon Bedrock](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/tokenomics-at-scale-how-jamf-built-real-time-spend-enforcement-for-amazon-bedrock/) — The template for governing AI cost at scale: tiered per-user budgets, automated enforcement via IAM policies and Lambda, with a deployable architecture teams can adapt immediately.
