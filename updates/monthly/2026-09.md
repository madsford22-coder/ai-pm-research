# September 2026 Research Summary

This month surfaced a consistent tension running through the AI product space: models keep getting better and cheaper, but adoption keeps getting harder. We watched OpenAI and Anthropic compress costs (40% cheaper Claude, new mid-tier GPT models), while simultaneously the gap between "capability" and "will people actually use this" became impossible to ignore. The real story wasn't model releases—it was that the infrastructure, governance, and trust layers needed to deploy AI at scale are still being built, usually by teams shipping in production without a blueprint.

The structural shift worth noting: AI moved from a model problem to a systems problem. Nobody's arguing about model capability anymore. The bottleneck is now memory management (how do agents remember?), eval methodology (how do you know if it's better?), auth and governance (who did what?), and the most human problem of all—making people actually want to use the thing instead of complying with a mandate to try it.

## What Matters

- **The cost floor keeps dropping, but the moat question stays hard.** Anthropic's Opus 5.5 dropped 40% below Opus 5. OpenAI launched Luna as a mid-tier model between expensive-and-frontier and cheap-but-limited. Pieter Levels published $25K/month in SaaS he replaced with vibe-coded alternatives at 1/250th the cost. The pattern is clear: if your defensibility was "we have engineers," that moat is eroding. The actual defensibility now comes from workflow specificity, switching costs, and genuine product insight—things AI doesn't commoditize. Julie Zhuo's observation that third-party software friction now exceeds build-it-yourself friction for many internal tools isn't theoretical anymore; it's the constraint PMs need to design around.

- **Agent infrastructure is consolidating around three hard problems: memory, evals, and governance.** LangSmith shipped red teaming, fine-tuning, and Trajectories (a readable view of agent sessions). Notion launched a skills library and published how they're storing agent memory as Notion pages. AWS published memory lifecycle policies as a CDK template. Teresa Torres published an AI evals guide specifically for product teams. The pattern is unanimous: we've moved past "can agents do this?" to "can we trust, measure, and govern agents at scale?" The teams solving these three problems—shared memory that's inspectable, evals that surface real failures, governance that enforces policy without manual review—are shipping the infrastructure everyone else needs. This is the unglamorous work that determines whether agentic products actually get deployed.

- **Enterprise AI adoption has a playbook now, but most orgs skip the first two steps.** Stripe's engineering team published how they scaled AI across the company through three layers: context (shared knowledge agents can access), governance (policies for what AI can and can't do), and shared skills (capabilities teams can reuse). Google consolidated Vertex AI into the Gemini Agent Platform. Cursor shipped self-hosted machines so enterprises can run agents without sending IP to the cloud. The signal is clear: enterprise adoption isn't blocked by model capability or even cost. It's blocked by the unglamorous infrastructure—where does data live? Who can do what? How do we audit it? Stripe's framework is a useful diagnostic: most orgs get halfway through (they maybe have governance) and then stall. The PMs winning are the ones who treat this as a product architecture problem, not a change management problem.
