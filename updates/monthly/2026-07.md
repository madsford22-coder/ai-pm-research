# July 2026 Research Summary

The central tension of July is the gap between what AI products claim and what they actually deliver when users encounter them in real conditions. Lenny's tech worker sentiment survey reveals a workforce splitting into those who've mastered AI tools and those who haven't—not because of talent differences, but because reliability and onboarding matter. Meanwhile, Ben Tossell's breakdown of Perplexity's failures (broken reservations, location-specific features that don't work) and the broader pattern across multiple teams shows us that demos don't survive contact with reality.

The work that matters right now isn't making AI faster or cheaper—it's making it reliable enough that users can actually trust it. That shift changes everything about how you prioritize features, how you design for failure, and what "done" means.

## What Matters

- **The AI adoption gap is a product problem, not just a training problem.** Lenny's sentiment survey shows tech workers splitting into thriving and struggling cohorts along AI capability lines, and the gap is widening. But the people thriving aren't systematically smarter—many are just earlier adopters who got onboarded better. This matters because it tells you that your floor-raising features (better documentation, clearer examples, gentler first experiences) are worth prioritizing alongside ceiling-raising ones. Teams that shipped good onboarding for AI tools are seeing wider adoption; teams that let power users drive the narrative are left with unequal capability distribution inside their own orgs.

- **Agent scaffolding is becoming a PM discipline.** LangChain showed they matched Anthropic Opus performance using Nemotron 3 Ultra at 8x lower cost—by tuning only the harness, not the model. Lenny then published a concrete Sentry bug-triage harness he built himself as a PM, walking through the exact prompts and context structure. This signals that the bottleneck for AI product quality is shifting from "which model" to "how is the loop structured." PMs who understand harness design—the scaffolding that controls what agents see, decide, and do—will be able to have much more useful conversations with engineering about where to invest to improve performance.

- **Reliability and positioning are inseparable now.** Perplexity's assistant promises reservation booking and reminders, but those features fail silently in real-world conditions (wrong locations, incorrect times). The product positioning created expectations that the feature couldn't meet. This happens because teams ship capabilities when they're available enough to demo, not when they're reliable enough to deliver on the promise. For any agentic feature you're shipping, the hard part isn't building it—it's deciding whether the reliability level you've actually achieved justifies the claim you're making in onboarding or marketing. Shipping an agent feature that "sometimes works" under the guise that it "works" trades trust for novelty.

## Essential Resources

2. **LangChain** — [Tuning the Harness, Not the Model: A Nemotron 3 Ultra Playbook](https://www.langchain.com/blog/tuning-the-harness-not-the-model-a-nemotron-3-ultra-playbook) — Matched Opus 4.8 agent performance using a cheaper model by optimizing only the scaffolding and control flow, demonstrating that harness design is often the real lever for quality improvement.

3. **Ethan Mollick** — [The Twilight of the Chatbots](https://www.oneusefulthing.org/p/the-twilight-of-the-chatbots) — Argues convincingly that task completion (agents that do the work) is superseding task assistance (AI that helps you do the work) as the competitive frame, which changes everything about UX and trust design.
